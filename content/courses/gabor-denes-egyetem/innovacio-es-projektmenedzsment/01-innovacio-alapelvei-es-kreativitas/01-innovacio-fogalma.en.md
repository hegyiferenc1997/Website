## What you will learn

- what **innovation** means and how it differs from **invention**,
- what **Joseph Schumpeter** said about innovation, and what the **Oslo Manual** is,
- the **sources** innovations come from.

## Invention and innovation

The two words are often mixed up, but they mean different things:

| Concept        | Meaning                                                                       | Example                                         |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **invention**  | the **first creation** of a new idea, process or device                       | the invention of the transistor in a lab (1947) |
| **innovation** | the **practical, market introduction** of the new idea that **creates value** | transistor pocket radios appearing in shops     |

In short: **innovation = idea + implementation + success on the market (or in the organisation)**. An invention becomes an innovation when someone uses it and benefits from it.

> Many brilliant inventions never became innovations because they never found their users – and many successful innovations contain no new invention at all, only a novel combination of existing parts (e.g. the smartphone).

## Schumpeter and "creative destruction"

According to the Austrian economist **Joseph Alois Schumpeter** (1883–1950), the engine of economic development is the **entrepreneur** who carries out new combinations. He distinguished five kinds:

1. a new product (or a new quality of a product),
2. a new method of production,
3. the opening of a new market,
4. a new source of raw materials or components,
5. a new form of organisation (e.g. creating or breaking up a monopoly).

He described innovation as **creative destruction**: the new solution pushes out the old. Digital cameras "destroyed" the film industry, streaming destroyed DVD rental shops.

## The Oslo Manual

The **Oslo Manual**, a joint publication of the **OECD** and **Eurostat**, gives the internationally accepted, statistical definition of innovation. According to the 2018 (4th) edition:

> An innovation is a **new or improved** product or process (or combination thereof) that **differs significantly** from the unit's previous products or processes and that has been **made available** to potential users (product) or **brought into use** by the unit (process).

Its key elements are novelty (at least new to the organisation), significant difference and **implementation** – a plan left in a drawer is not an innovation.

## Levels of novelty

An innovation can be new

- to the **organisation** (we have not had this before),
- to the **market** (nobody offered it on our market yet),
- to the **world** (nobody, anywhere, has done it before).

## Where do innovations come from?

**Peter Drucker** named seven sources, including the unexpected success or failure, weak points of a process, market and demographic changes, changes in perception and **new knowledge** (research and development).

Two basic driving forces:

- **technology push**: new knowledge or technology looks for an application (e.g. the laser),
- **market pull**: customer demand "pulls" the solution into existence (e.g. longer battery life).

## Summary

- Invention = first creation of a new idea; innovation = its successful, value-creating implementation.
- For Schumpeter, innovation drives "creative destruction" and comes in five kinds.
- The Oslo Manual (OECD) gives the international definition: a new or significantly improved, **implemented** product or process.
- Innovation can be driven by technology push or market pull.
