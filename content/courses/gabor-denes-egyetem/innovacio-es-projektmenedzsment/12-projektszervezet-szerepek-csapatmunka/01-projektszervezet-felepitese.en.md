## What you will learn

- how a project fits into the company's **organisational structure**,
- the difference between **functional**, **matrix** and **projectised** organisations,
- how much **authority the project manager** has in each,
- what a **project management office** (PMO) is.

## Why does the organisational form matter?

Project staff usually come from the company's **line organisation** (departments). The organisational form decides **whom they obey**: their department head or the project manager – and this strongly affects how far the PM can lead.

## Functional organisation

The company is built around **specialisms** (development, marketing, finance …). A project runs within one department, or through agreement between department heads.

```text
               CEO
      ┌──────────┼──────────┐
  Development Marketing  Finance
   ●  ●  ●     ●  ●       ●  ●     ← project participants stay in their departments
```

- ✅ professional depth, clear career paths, good resource utilisation,
- ❌ the PM has **little authority** (often just a coordinator), slow cross-department coordination, the department's daily work takes priority.

## Projectised organisation

The company is built around **projects**; the project manager is the team's **full** leader, and members work on the project full time (e.g. construction firms, consultancies).

```text
               CEO
      ┌──────────┼──────────┐
  Project 1   Project 2   Project 3
   PM ●●●      PM ●●       PM ●●●●
```

- ✅ the PM has **full authority**, fast decisions, strong team identity,
- ❌ expensive (duplicated resources), "where do people go when the project ends?", professional isolation.

## Matrix organisation

A **mix of the two**: team members report to **two bosses** – to the department head professionally and to the PM on project tasks.

| Matrix type  | PM authority | Characteristic                                                    |
| ------------ | ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **weak**     | low          | the PM is more of a coordinator, department heads decide          |
| **balanced** | moderate     | the PM and department heads share power                           |
| **strong**   | high         | the PM decides, often a member of a project management department |

- ✅ flexible use of resources, professional background preserved, good for many parallel projects,
- ❌ **two bosses** – conflicts over priorities, more complex communication.

### Summary table

| Aspect                 | Functional    | Weak matrix | Balanced  | Strong matrix | Projectised |
| ---------------------- | ------------- | ----------- | --------- | ------------- | ----------- |
| PM authority           | little / none | low         | moderate  | high          | full        |
| who manages the budget | dept. head    | dept. head  | mixed     | PM            | PM          |
| PM's time              | part time     | part time   | full time | full time     | full time   |

## The project management office (PMO)

A **PMO** (Project Management Office) is an organisational unit that provides project management **standards, methods and support**. Its three types (PMBOK):

| Type            | Degree of control | What does it do?                                     |
| --------------- | ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **supportive**  | low               | provides templates, best practices, training         |
| **controlling** | moderate          | prescribes the methodology, audits, requires reports |
| **directive**   | high              | runs the projects itself; PMs report to the PMO      |

The PMO is often also responsible for **portfolio management**: which project should start, and how to allocate resources across projects (topic 4).

## Summary

- Functional: organised by specialism, the PM has little authority.
- Projectised: organised by project, the PM has full authority.
- Matrix (weak, balanced, strong): two bosses, PM authority depends on the type.
- PMO: supportive, controlling, directive; standards, methods, portfolio.
