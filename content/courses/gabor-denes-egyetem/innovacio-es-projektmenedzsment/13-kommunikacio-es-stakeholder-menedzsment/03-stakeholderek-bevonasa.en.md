## What you will learn

- how to assess how **engaged** stakeholders are,
- how to plan their **engagement**,
- why people **resist** change and what you can do,
- the basics of **principled negotiation**.

## Levels of engagement

PMBOK distinguishes five levels:

| Level          | Characteristic                                     |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **unaware**    | does not know about the project and its impact     |
| **resistant**  | knows about it but opposes the change              |
| **neutral**    | knows about it but neither supports nor opposes it |
| **supportive** | knows about it and supports it                     |
| **leading**    | knows about it and actively works for its success  |

## The engagement assessment matrix

For each key stakeholder we mark the **current** (**C**) and the **desired** (**D**) level. The gap between them shows where we need to act:

| Stakeholder        | Unaware | Resistant | Neutral | Supportive | Leading |
| ------------------ | ------- | --------- | ------- | ---------- | ------- |
| sponsor (rector)   |         |           |         | C          | D       |
| registrar's office |         | C         |         | D          |         |
| lecturers          | C       |           | D       |            |         |
| student union      |         |           | C       | D          |         |

In this example the **registrar's office** is the biggest challenge: it must move from resistant to supportive – and it has a lot of influence.

## Engagement strategies

- **Inform**: regular, clear information on the project's purpose and impact (moves people out of "unaware").
- **Consult**: asking for opinions, surveys, interviews – and responding to the feedback!
- **Involve**: taking part in workshops, testing, key-user roles.
- **Collaborate**: joint decision making, shared responsibility.
- **Empower**: the stakeholder leads part of the change themselves (they become a "change champion").

## Causes of resistance and how to handle it

People rarely hate the change itself – rather what they might **lose** through it. Common causes:

- **fear** of the unknown, of losing their job or status,
- **more work** during the transition,
- being **left out** of decisions ("about us, without us"),
- **bad experience** with earlier projects,
- the benefits are **not clear**.

How to handle it:

1. **listen** – resistance is often justified and carries useful information,
2. explain the **"why"**, show the personal benefits,
3. **involve** them in planning and testing,
4. **training** and support during the transition,
5. show **quick wins**.

Organisational change management will come up again in topic 14 (Kotter, ADKAR).

## Principled negotiation

The four principles of the **Harvard negotiation method** (Fisher and Ury: "Getting to Yes", 1981):

1. **Separate the people from the problem** – hard on the problem, soft on the people.
2. **Focus on interests, not positions** – behind "I want this" there is a "why".
3. **Invent options for mutual gain** – first collect options, decide only afterwards.
4. **Insist on objective criteria** – market price, standards, data.

A key concept is **BATNA** (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement): your best alternative if no agreement is reached. The better your BATNA, the stronger your negotiating position; an agreement worse than your BATNA is not worth accepting.

## Summary

- Engagement levels: unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading.
- Engagement matrix: current (C) and desired (D) level – the gap is the to-do.
- Strategies: inform, consult, involve, collaborate, empower.
- Resistance: listen, explain why, involve, train, quick wins.
- Principled negotiation: separate people and problem, interests, mutual gain, objective criteria; BATNA.
