## What you will learn

- who counts as a **stakeholder**,
- how to **identify** stakeholders,
- what the **stakeholder register** contains,
- how to **analyse** them (power–interest grid, salience model).

## Who is a stakeholder?

A **stakeholder** is any individual, group or organisation that **affects** the project, or is **affected** by it (or perceives itself to be). They can be

- **internal**: sponsor, management, project team, other departments, operations,
- **external**: client, users, suppliers, authorities, trade unions, the media, the local community,
- **supporters** and **opponents** alike.

> When introducing a university student information system, stakeholders include the rector (sponsor), the registrar's office, lecturers, students, the student union, the IT department, the software supplier and the data protection officer.

## Identifying stakeholders

- **brainstorming** with the team: "who is affected by the change, and who can influence it?",
- reviewing the project charter, contracts and the **organisation chart**,
- **interviews**: "Who else should I talk to?" (snowball method),
- **lessons learned** from similar earlier projects.

Identification is **not one-off**: new stakeholders may appear during the project, and others may drop out.

## The stakeholder register

The **stakeholder register** records for every stakeholder:

| Field                    | Example                                        |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| name, organisation, role | Dr Anna Kovács, head of the registrar's office |
| expectations, interests  | less admin work, flawless data migration       |
| influence (power)        | high – approves the processes                  |
| interest                 | high                                           |
| current attitude         | neutral, a little worried                      |
| communication needs      | a short weekly check-in                        |

> The register may contain **sensitive** information (e.g. "opposes the project") – treat it confidentially.

## The power–interest grid (Mendelow)

The most widely used analysis places stakeholders on two axes: how much **power** (influence) they have and how much **interest** they have in the project.

```text
 power ▲
  high │ ┌──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┐
       │ │  KEEP SATISFIED      │  MANAGE CLOSELY      │
       │ │                      │                      │
       │ │  e.g. finance director│  e.g. sponsor       │
       │ ├──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┤
       │ │  MONITOR             │  KEEP INFORMED       │
       │ │                      │                      │
  low  │ │  e.g. distant depts. │  e.g. users          │
       │ └──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┘
       └─────────────────────────────────────────────────▶ interest
          low                                       high
```

- **High power, high interest** → **manage closely**: involve them in decisions, consult regularly.
- **High power, low interest** → **keep satisfied**: brief them on the essentials, do not overload them.
- **Low power, high interest** → **keep informed**: regular news, a way to give feedback.
- **Low power, low interest** → **monitor**: minimal effort, but watch whether their position changes.

## The salience model

The model of **Mitchell, Agle and Wood** (1997) looks at three attributes:

- **power** – can they impose their will,
- **legitimacy** – is their claim justified,
- **urgency** – how immediate is their demand for attention.

A stakeholder with **all three** is a **definitive** stakeholder – they get the most attention. E.g. an authority that, by law (legitimate), with an immediate deadline (urgent), can stop the project (power).

## Summary

- Stakeholder: anyone who affects or is affected by the project; internal, external, supporter, opponent.
- Identification: brainstorming, documents, interviews – continuously.
- Stakeholder register: role, expectations, influence, interest, attitude, communication needs.
- Power–interest grid: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor.
- Salience model: power, legitimacy, urgency; all three = definitive stakeholder.

## Lesson exercise

```quiz required
? According to the power–interest grid, what do we do with a stakeholder with **high power and high interest**?
+ manage them closely (involve them in decisions)
- just monitor them
- keep them informed
- keep them satisfied but do not overload them
! Manage closely.

? Users are very interested but have little decision-making power. Which strategy fits them?
- manage closely
- keep satisfied
+ keep informed
- monitor
! Keep informed: regular news, a way to give feedback.

? Who is **not** a stakeholder when introducing a university student information system?
- the students
- the lecturers
- the software supplier
+ another country's national football team
! A stakeholder affects or is affected by the project.

? Which three attributes does the salience model examine?
+ power, legitimacy, urgency
- time, cost, scope
- strengths, weaknesses, opportunities
- planning, execution, control
! Mitchell, Agle and Wood (1997).

? Why must stakeholder identification be repeated **continuously** during the project?
- because the standard requires a daily update
+ because new stakeholders may appear, and existing ones' attitude and influence may change
- because the register is lost every week
- it need not be repeated; once at the start is enough
! The project environment changes.
```
