## What you will learn

- how a new technology **spreads** among users (Rogers),
- what **the chasm** is and why many start-ups fail there,
- what the **TRL** (technology readiness level) scale measures,
- in which steps to **introduce** a new technology in an organisation.

## Diffusion of innovations (Rogers)

According to **Everett Rogers** (1962), the adopters of a new idea fall into five groups, as a percentage of the whole market:

| Group              | Share | Characteristic                         |
| ------------------ | ----- | -------------------------------------- |
| **innovators**     | 2.5%  | risk-taking technology enthusiasts     |
| **early adopters** | 13.5% | opinion leaders, visionaries           |
| **early majority** | 34%   | pragmatists, want proof and references |
| **late majority**  | 34%   | sceptics, switch when they have to     |
| **laggards**       | 16%   | stick to the old                       |

The shares add up to 100%. Rogers names five factors that **speed up** adoption: **relative advantage** (how much better than the old), **compatibility** (how well it fits existing habits), **simplicity**, **trialability** and **observability** (whether others' results are visible).

## The chasm (Geoffrey Moore)

According to **Geoffrey Moore**'s book "Crossing the Chasm" (1991), there is a **chasm** between early adopters and the early majority: visionaries buy novelty, but pragmatists want a **complete, reliable solution proven by references**. Many tech products get stuck here.

The way across is to fully win a **narrow niche** (a _beachhead_): solve every problem of a well-defined customer group, then move on using their references.

## Technology readiness levels (TRL)

The **TRL** (_Technology Readiness Level_) scale was developed by **NASA**; today EU research programmes (e.g. Horizon Europe) use it too. Its nine levels:

| TRL | Level                                                         |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | basic principles observed                                     |
| 2   | technology concept formulated                                 |
| 3   | experimental proof of concept                                 |
| 4   | technology validated in the lab                               |
| 5   | technology validated in a relevant environment                |
| 6   | technology demonstrated in a relevant environment (prototype) |
| 7   | system prototype demonstrated in an operational environment   |
| 8   | system complete and qualified                                 |
| 9   | actual system proven in operational environment               |

The lower the TRL, the **higher the technical risk** – we take this into account when evaluating innovations in topic 4.

## Introducing a new technology in an organisation

1. **Need and goal** – what problem does it solve? (Start from the business goal, not from the technology.)
2. **Assessment** – market solutions, costs, compatibility with existing systems.
3. **Pilot project** – try it in a small, controlled group and measure the results.
4. **Decision and roll-out** – gradually, on a schedule; training, documentation.
5. **Follow-up** – measure the benefits (KPIs, topic 8) and improve.

> The human side is at least as important as the technical one: users' **resistance** is handled through training, involvement and showing the benefits (change management, topic 14).

## Summary

- Rogers: innovators 2.5%, early adopters 13.5%, early majority 34%, late majority 34%, laggards 16%.
- The chasm lies between early adopters and the early majority; you cross it by winning a narrow niche.
- TRL 1–9: from basic principles to a system proven in operation.
- Introduction: need → assessment → pilot → gradual roll-out → follow-up.
