## What you will learn

- what an **innovation strategy** is and which questions it answers,
- how to build a **SWOT analysis**,
- which **strategy types** a company can choose from (Freeman),
- how to use the **Ansoff matrix** and the **blue ocean** idea.

## What is an innovation strategy?

An **innovation strategy** is part of corporate strategy: a long-term plan of **in which areas, to what extent, with what resources and at what pace** the company innovates to gain competitive advantage. Its main questions:

- **Where** do we innovate? (product, process, business model)
- **How boldly?** (an incremental or radical portfolio)
- **When?** (are we first to market, or do we follow?)
- **How?** (own R&D, partnerships, acquisitions, open innovation)

## SWOT analysis

Strategy development starts with situation analysis. The four fields of **SWOT**:

|              | **Helpful**       | **Harmful**    |
| ------------ | ----------------- | -------------- |
| **Internal** | **S**trengths     | **W**eaknesses |
| **External** | **O**pportunities | **T**hreats    |

- Strengths and weaknesses are **inside the organisation** (e.g. a strong dev team, an outdated code base).
- Opportunities and threats come from the **environment** (e.g. a new EU grant, a new competitor).

Strategies can be derived from SWOT (the **TOWS matrix**): e.g. an **S–O** strategy = use our strengths to seize the opportunity.

## Freeman's innovation strategy types

**Christopher Freeman** described six types:

| Strategy        | Characteristic                                                                        |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **offensive**   | first to market with new technology; strong own R&D, high risk                        |
| **defensive**   | quickly follows the pioneer, learns from its mistakes, comes with an improved version |
| **imitative**   | copies proven solutions at lower cost                                                 |
| **dependent**   | develops to the needs of a larger partner (e.g. a customer), as a supplier            |
| **traditional** | hardly innovates, keeps producing its existing products                               |
| **opportunist** | finds a narrow market niche and exploits it quickly                                   |

> **First mover advantage** does not always hold: a fast follower (e.g. Google versus earlier search engines) is often more successful.

## The Ansoff matrix

**Igor Ansoff**'s matrix shows the **direction of growth** by the novelty of product and market:

|                     | **existing product** | **new product**     |
| ------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| **existing market** | market penetration   | product development |
| **new market**      | market development   | diversification     |

Risk grows from the top-left towards the bottom-right: **diversification** (new product on a new market) is the riskiest.

## Blue ocean strategy

According to **W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne** (2005), in a **red ocean** companies fight for the same customers on the same market ("the water is red with blood"). A **blue ocean** strategy instead creates a **new, uncontested market space** by **lowering cost** and **adding new value** at the same time.

Its tool is the **four actions framework** (ERRC): what to **Eliminate**, what to **Reduce**, what to **Raise** and what to **Create**?

Example: the Nintendo Wii did not compete on graphics performance; it won a new customer group with motion-controlled family gaming.

## Summary

- An innovation strategy states where, how much, when and how the company innovates.
- SWOT: internal strengths and weaknesses, external opportunities and threats.
- Freeman: offensive, defensive, imitative, dependent, traditional, opportunist strategy.
- Ansoff: market penetration, product development, market development, diversification (riskiest).
- Blue ocean: a new, uncontested market space, the ERRC framework.
