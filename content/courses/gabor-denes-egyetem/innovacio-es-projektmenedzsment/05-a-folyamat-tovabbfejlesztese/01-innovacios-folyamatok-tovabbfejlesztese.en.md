## What you will learn

- what an organisation's **innovation process** looks like (the funnel),
- how innovation **process models** evolved (Rothwell),
- how to improve the process continuously with the **PDCA cycle** and **Kaizen**,
- which **metrics** measure innovation performance.

## The innovation funnel

An organisation starts from many ideas and filters them step by step until a few successful products remain:

```text
 ┌──────────────────────────────┐  idea generation (many ideas)
  \                            /
   \  screening, evaluation   /    (topic 4: scoring, Stage-Gate)
    \                        /
     \  development, test   /
      \                    /
       \  launch          /
        └──────────────┘          few, successful innovations
```

Improving the process aims to make the funnel **faster**, **cheaper** and **more accurate**: good ideas should not get lost, and bad ones should drop out as early as possible.

## Rothwell's five generations

According to **Roy Rothwell** (1994), innovation process models evolved over five generations:

| Generation | Model                               | Essence                                                           |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1st        | **technology push**                 | linear: research → development → production → market              |
| 2nd        | **market pull**                     | linear, but starts from customer need                             |
| 3rd        | **coupling (interactive)**          | interaction of research and market, with feedback loops           |
| 4th        | **integrated (parallel)**           | departments work in parallel, together (e.g. Japanese car makers) |
| 5th        | **networked (systems integration)** | a network of partners, digital tools, rapid iteration             |

Its modern continuation is **open innovation** (topic 1) and **agile** development (topic 11).

## The PDCA cycle

The basic model of continuous improvement is the **Deming cycle** (W. Edwards Deming, after Walter Shewhart):

```text
        ┌──────────┐
        │  PLAN    │  plan: goal, cause, action
        └────┬─────┘
 ┌──────┐    │    ┌──────┐
 │ ACT  │◄───┼───►│  DO  │  do: small-scale trial
 └──────┘    │    └──────┘
 act:        │
 standardise or adjust
        ┌────┴─────┐
        │  CHECK   │  check: measure the result
        └──────────┘
```

1. **Plan** – define the problem and the goal, analyse causes, plan the action.
2. **Do** – try it on a small scale.
3. **Check** – measure the results and compare them with the goal.
4. **Act** – if it worked, **standardise** it; if not, adjust it and start the cycle again.

## Kaizen

**Kaizen** (Japanese for "change for the better") is the philosophy of **continuous improvement in small steps**, involving **everyone** – from managers to machine operators. Its opposite is the rare, big-leap **innovation breakthrough** (Kairyo). The two complement each other: after a breakthrough, Kaizen polishes the new process.

> In a software team the typical form of Kaizen is the **retrospective**: at the end of every sprint we discuss what went well, what did not, and what we change in the next sprint (topic 11).

## Innovation metrics

What is not measured is hard to improve. Common metrics:

- **time-to-market**: the time from idea to launch,
- **idea conversion rate**: what percentage of submitted ideas reach launch,
- **share of revenue from new products**: revenue from products launched in the last (e.g. 3) years,
- **R&D spending** as a percentage of revenue,
- **innovation ROI**.

Example: 6 out of 200 submitted ideas reach the market → idea conversion rate **3%**.

## Summary

- Innovation funnel: many ideas → screening → development → few successful innovations.
- Rothwell: technology push → market pull → coupling → integrated → networked model.
- PDCA: Plan – Do – Check – Act; a working solution is standardised.
- Kaizen: continuous, small, all-inclusive improvement.
- Metrics: time-to-market, conversion rate, revenue share of new products, R&D ratio.
