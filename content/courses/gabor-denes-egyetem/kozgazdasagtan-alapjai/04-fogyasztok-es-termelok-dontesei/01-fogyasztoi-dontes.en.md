## What you will learn

- what **utility** and **marginal utility** are,
- why marginal utility **diminishes**,
- what the **budget constraint** shows,
- the rule of the **consumer optimum**,
- what **consumer surplus** is.

## Utility and marginal utility

**Utility** is the satisfaction that consuming a product gives. It cannot be measured precisely, but in the model we express it in "points" (utils).

- **Total utility (TU):** the combined utility of all units consumed.
- **Marginal utility (MU):** the **extra** utility from the next unit.

**The law of diminishing marginal utility:** the more of a product we consume, the smaller the utility of an additional unit.

| Energy drinks (per day) | 1   | 2   | 3   | 4   | 5   |
| ----------------------- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total utility (TU)      | 30  | 50  | 60  | 64  | 62  |
| Marginal utility (MU)   | 30  | 20  | 10  | 4   | −2  |

The fifth drink is already harmful (negative marginal utility). This is why we pay little for water even though it is vital: there is so much of it that the marginal utility of the last glass is small (**the paradox of value**: water and diamonds).

## The budget constraint

The consumer is limited by **income and prices**. For two products (X and Y):

$$ P_X \cdot X + P_Y \cdot Y \le I $$

**Example:** you have 10,000 HUF a month for entertainment. A cinema ticket ($X$) costs 2,000 HUF, a coffee ($Y$) 1,000 HUF. At most 5 films **or** 10 coffees, or combinations in between (e.g. 3 films + 4 coffees).

- If **income rises**, the constraint shifts out in parallel.
- If **one product becomes more expensive**, the constraint pivots inwards on that axis.

## The consumer optimum

A rational consumer allocates their money so that the marginal utility of the **last forint** spent is **the same** for every product:

$$ \frac{MU_X}{P_X} = \frac{MU_Y}{P_Y} $$

**Why?** If 1 HUF brings 0.02 utils at the cinema and 0.03 utils on coffee, moving one forint from cinema to coffee raises total utility. It is worth reallocating until the ratios are equal (diminishing marginal utility ensures this happens).

## Consumer surplus

**Consumer surplus** is the difference between what the buyer **would have been willing to pay** and what they **actually pay**.

**Example:** you would pay at most 450,000 HUF for a new laptop, and you buy it on sale for 380,000 HUF → your consumer surplus is **70,000 HUF**.

On a market, consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price. The lower the price, the larger it is.

## Behavioural aspects

Real consumers do not always "optimise":

- **anchoring**: the crossed-out "original" price makes the sale look like a better deal,
- **default effect**: few people cancel an automatically renewing subscription,
- **present bias**: we would rather spend today than think about the future (topic 5, saving).

## Summary

- Marginal utility is the extra utility of the next unit, and it usually diminishes.
- Budget constraint: $P_X X + P_Y Y \le I$.
- Optimum: $MU_X/P_X = MU_Y/P_Y$ – the last forint is worth the same everywhere.
- Consumer surplus = willingness to pay − price.
