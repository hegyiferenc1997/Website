## What you will learn

- what **revenue**, **marginal revenue** and **profit** are,
- the profit-maximisation rule: **MR = MC**,
- the features of the four main **market structures**,
- why firms are tempted to **collude**, and why it is hard (prisoner's dilemma).

## Revenue, marginal revenue, profit

- **Total revenue (TR):** $TR = P \cdot Q$.
- **Marginal revenue (MR):** the **extra** revenue from selling the next unit.
- **Profit:** $\pi = TR - TC$.

**The profit-maximisation rule:** the firm increases output as long as **MR ≥ MC**; profit is highest where **MR = MC** (an application of marginal analysis – topic 1).

| Q   | P (thousand HUF) | TR  | TC  | MR  | MC  | Profit |
| --- | ---------------- | --- | --- | --- | --- | ------ |
| 1   | 100              | 100 | 60  | 100 | –   | 40     |
| 2   | 90               | 180 | 100 | 80  | 40  | 80     |
| 3   | 80               | 240 | 150 | 60  | 50  | 90     |
| 4   | 70               | 280 | 210 | 40  | 60  | 70     |
| 5   | 60               | 300 | 280 | 20  | 70  | 20     |

For the 3rd unit MR (60) > MC (50) still holds; for the 4th MR (40) < MC (60). **Optimum: Q = 3, profit = 90.**

## The four market structures

|                    | Perfect competition               | Monopolistic competition        | Oligopoly                             | Monopoly                           |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| number of sellers  | very many                         | many                            | a few                                 | one                                |
| product            | identical                         | differentiated                  | identical or differentiated           | no close substitute                |
| entry              | free                              | easy                            | difficult                             | impossible or very difficult       |
| influence on price | none (price taker)                | small                           | significant, mutual dependence        | large (price maker)                |
| example            | wheat, currency on a large market | restaurants, mobile apps, cafés | mobile carriers, smartphone OS, cloud | local water utility, patented drug |

### Perfect competition

Many small sellers, identical products: none can influence the price, so **P = MR**. Because of free entry, in the long run **economic profit falls to zero** (if there is profit, new firms enter and push the price down). This is the benchmark of efficiency.

### Monopoly

A single seller. The barrier to entry may be **legal** (patent, licence), **natural** (it does not pay to build a water network twice – a **natural monopoly**), or **network effects** (topic 12).

The monopolist **chooses the price**: it can only sell more by lowering the price, so **MR < P**. Because of the MR = MC rule it **produces less at a higher price** than a competitive market → a loss to society (**deadweight loss**). That is why **competition law** exists (in Hungary the Competition Authority, GVH; in the EU the European Commission; e.g. the EU Digital Markets Act, DMA, for large platforms).

### Oligopoly and the prisoner's dilemma

A few large firms whose decisions **affect each other**. If they collude (**cartel** – illegal), they can behave like a monopoly. But each of them has an incentive to "cheat".

**Prisoner's dilemma** – two mobile carriers decide on prices (profit in billion HUF, A; B):

|                   | B: high price | B: low price |
| ----------------- | ------------- | ------------ |
| **A: high price** | 10; 10        | 2; 14        |
| **A: low price**  | 14; 2         | 5; 5         |

Whatever the other does, the **low price** is better for each (14 > 10 and 5 > 2) – this is the **dominant strategy**. So both end up at 5; 5, even though 10; 10 would be better for both. Customers win.

### Monopolistic competition

Many sellers, but each product is a little **different** (brand, features, location). There can be profit in the short run, but easy entry melts it away in the long run. Typical tools are **advertising** and **product differentiation** (e.g. in app stores).

## Summary

- Profit maximum: MR = MC.
- Perfect competition: price takers, P = MR, zero economic profit in the long run.
- Monopoly: lower quantity, higher price, deadweight loss → competition law.
- Oligopoly: mutual dependence; the prisoner's dilemma makes cartels hard to sustain.
- Monopolistic competition: differentiated products, easy entry.
