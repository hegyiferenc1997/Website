## What you will learn

- what an **economic model** is and what **ceteris paribus** means,
- the difference between **positive** and **normative** statements,
- why **correlation** and **causation** are not the same,
- how to calculate with **percent, percentage points and indices**,
- the difference between **nominal** and **real** values.

## Models and ceteris paribus

A **model** is a simplified copy of reality: it keeps only the factors that matter for the question studied – like a map that does not show every tree.

**Ceteris paribus** (Latin: "all else being equal"): we study the effect of one factor at a time. "If the ticket price rises, ceteris paribus fewer tickets are bought" – assuming that incomes, the prices of other entertainment etc. do not change in the meantime.

## Positive and normative statements

| Positive statement                                              | Normative statement                          |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| describes **what is** – can be checked against data             | a value judgement, says **what ought to be** |
| "Raising the minimum wage reduces the employment of beginners." | "The minimum wage should be raised."         |
| "Inflation was 4% last year."                                   | "Inflation is too high."                     |

Economists support decisions with positive analysis; the normative decision belongs to society and politics.

## Correlation and causation

If two series move together (**correlation**), it does not follow that one causes the other.

- In summer more ice cream is sold and there are more beach accidents. Ice cream does not cause accidents – both are caused by **hot weather** (a hidden third factor).
- Reverse causation is also a trap: many police officers do not cause crime; more police are sent where there is more crime.

## Percent and percentage points

- **Percentage change:** $\dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \cdot 100$.
- **Percentage point:** the difference between two **values given in percent**.

**Example:** the central bank's base rate rises from 4% to 6%.

- The change is **2 percentage points**.
- Expressed in percent, however, it is a **50%** increase: $(6 - 4)/4 = 0.5$.

## Index

An **index** shows a value relative to a base, where the base = 100.

$$ \text{index} = \frac{\text{current period}}{\text{base period}} \cdot 100 $$

If a laptop costs 300,000 HUF in 2025 and 330,000 HUF in 2026, the price index is $330/300 \cdot 100 = 110$, i.e. a **10%** price rise.

Successive changes **multiply**, they do not add up: after a 10% increase and then a 10% decrease, $1.1 \cdot 0.9 = 0.99$, so the price is **1% lower** than originally.

## Nominal and real values

- **Nominal** value: measured at current prices, in forints.
- **Real** value: adjusted for price changes (inflation), i.e. how much **purchasing power** it represents.

Approximate rule for small values: **real change ≈ nominal change − inflation**.

Exactly: $\text{real index} = \dfrac{\text{nominal index}}{\text{price index}} \cdot 100$.

**Example:** your wage rises by 8%, inflation is 5%. Your real wage rises by about **3%** (exactly $1.08/1.05 \approx 1.0286$, i.e. 2.86%). If your wage rises by 8% but inflation is 15%, your real wage **falls**, even though you get more forints.

## Summary

- A model simplifies; ceteris paribus changes one factor at a time.
- Positive: what is (checkable); normative: what ought to be (value judgement).
- Correlation ≠ causation (hidden factor, reverse direction).
- Percentage point: the difference between two rates; successive changes multiply.
- Real ≈ nominal − inflation.
