## What you will learn

- the exact definition of **GDP**,
- the **three methods** of measuring GDP, including the formula $C + I + G + (X - M)$,
- the difference between **nominal** and **real** GDP,
- what **GDP per capita**, **purchasing power parity** and **GNI** are,
- the **limitations** of GDP.

## What is GDP?

**Gross domestic product** (GDP) is the **market value** of all **final** goods and services produced within a country's territory in a given period (a quarter, a year).

Every word of the definition matters:

- **market value**: measured in forints at market prices, so a laptop and a haircut can be added up,
- **final**: only products sold to the final user – intermediate products (e.g. the chip built into the laptop) are not counted separately, otherwise we would count them **twice**,
- **produced**: sales of used goods (a second-hand phone) are not included, because they were counted earlier,
- **domestic**: within the country's territory – the output of a German car factory operating in Hungary is part of Hungarian GDP,
- **given period**: a flow, not a stock.

## Three ways of measuring

Because of the circular flow of income (topic 2), the three calculations in principle give the same result:

1. **Production approach:** the sum of all **value added** (the value of output minus the intermediate products used).
2. **Expenditure approach:** the sum of final uses.
3. **Income approach:** the sum of incomes from production (wages, profits, interest, rents) + taxes on production − subsidies + depreciation.

### An example of value added

| Actor        | Selling price | Intermediate product used | Value added                   |
| ------------ | ------------- | ------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| chip maker   | 50,000        | 0                         | 50,000                        |
| laptop maker | 200,000       | 50,000                    | 150,000                       |
| retailer     | 250,000       | 200,000                   | 50,000                        |
| **total**    |               |                           | **250,000** = the final price |

### The expenditure side

$$ GDP = C + I + G + (X - M) $$

| Term    | Meaning               | Example                                                    |
| ------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| $C$     | household consumption | food, phone, streaming                                     |
| $I$     | investment            | new factory, servers, housebuilding, change in inventories |
| $G$     | government purchases  | doctors' wages, roads (transfers are **not** included!)    |
| $X - M$ | net exports           | exports − imports                                          |

Pensions and family allowances are **not** part of $G$, because they are not purchases of goods or services but a redistribution of income (the goods bought with them appear in $C$).

Hungary is an open economy: both exports and imports are **around 80%** of GDP, so net exports are small, but the state of the world economy affects us very strongly.

## Nominal and real GDP

- **Nominal GDP:** at current prices – it can grow even if we produce nothing more, just because everything is more expensive.
- **Real GDP:** at the prices of a base year (constant prices) – it shows **actual output**. **Economic growth** is the change in real GDP.

$$ \text{real GDP} = \frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{GDP deflator}} \cdot 100 $$

If nominal GDP grows by 10% and the price level (deflator) by 6%, real growth is about **4%**.

## GDP per capita, PPP, GNI

- **GDP per capita** = GDP / population – a rough measure of living standards.
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP):** takes into account that prices differ between countries (a haircut is cheaper in Budapest than in Zurich). EU comparisons are made in PPP; on this basis Hungary's GDP per capita is about three quarters of the EU average.
- **GNI** (gross national income) = GDP + income received from abroad − income paid abroad. Where there are many foreign-owned firms (as in Hungary), part of the profits flows out, so **GNI is smaller than GDP**.

## What GDP does not show

- **household work** and voluntary work (cooking, raising children, open-source development),
- the **shadow economy** (it is estimated, but imprecisely),
- the **distribution of income** – the average can rise without the majority being better off,
- **environmental damage** (cleaning up pollution even increases GDP),
- **leisure**, health, happiness,
- the value of **free digital services** (search engines, maps, Wikipedia) – discussed in topic 12.

That is why there are complementary indicators, e.g. the **HDI** (topic 10).

## Summary

- GDP: the market value of final goods produced within a country's territory in a period.
- Three methods: production (value added), expenditure ($C + I + G + X - M$), income.
- Real GDP = at constant prices; growth is the change in real GDP.
- GDP per capita, PPP, GNI.
- GDP does not measure welfare, distribution, the environment or household work.
