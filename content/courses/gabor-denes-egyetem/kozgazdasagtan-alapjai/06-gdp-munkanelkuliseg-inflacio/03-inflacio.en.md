## What you will learn

- what **inflation** is and how the **consumer price index** measures it,
- what **core inflation** and the **GDP deflator** are,
- the main **causes** of inflation,
- the **costs** of inflation: whom it hurts and whom it helps,
- what **disinflation**, **deflation** and **hyperinflation** are.

## What is inflation?

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the **general price level** – not one product becoming more expensive, but the average level of prices. As a result, the **purchasing power** of money **falls**.

## The consumer price index (CPI)

Each month the Hungarian Central Statistical Office (KSH) compiles a **consumer basket**: several hundred goods and services that an average household buys (food, housing, energy, transport, clothing, telecommunications, entertainment…). Each item gets a **weight** according to how much households spend on it.

$$ \text{price index} = \frac{\text{price of the basket now}}{\text{price of the basket in the base period}} \cdot 100 $$

$$ \text{inflation (\%)} = \text{price index} - 100 $$

**Example:** the basket cost 200,000 HUF last year and 212,000 HUF now → the index is 106, inflation is **6%**.

### Measurement problems

- **Quality change:** this year's laptop costs the same but is twice as fast – it has actually become cheaper.
- **Substitution:** if butter becomes more expensive, more people buy margarine; a fixed basket follows this late.
- **New products:** the basket takes them in slowly.
- **Personal inflation:** a student's basket (rent, food, transport) differs from a pensioner's (medicines, utilities), so their inflation also differs from the average.

## Core inflation and the GDP deflator

- **Core inflation:** the CPI without items with volatile prices (fresh food, energy, regulated prices). It shows the **persistent** inflation trend better – the central bank watches it too.
- **GDP deflator:** the ratio of nominal to real GDP; it includes the prices of **all** domestically produced goods (investment goods too), but not imports.

## Causes of inflation

1. **Demand-pull inflation** ("too much money chasing too few goods"): demand grows faster than output – e.g. credit-financed spending, generous government spending, rapid wage growth.
2. **Cost-push inflation:** production becomes more expensive and firms pass it on – e.g. an energy price shock, supply chain disruptions, a weaker forint (more expensive imports).
3. **Expectations:** if everyone expects further price rises, workers demand bigger pay rises and firms raise prices in advance → a **wage-price spiral**. That is why it matters that the central bank is **credible**.
4. **In the long run:** if the money supply grows much faster than output, the price level rises (quantity theory of money, topic 7).

**Hungarian example:** in 2022–2023 several factors acted at once (an energy price shock due to the war, a weak forint, strong demand), and inflation rose **above 25%** in early 2023 – the highest in the EU.

## Whom does it hurt, whom does it help?

- **It hurts** people on fixed incomes (if pensions and scholarships do not keep up with prices), **savers** (if the interest rate is below inflation – a negative real rate), and **lenders**.
- **It helps** **borrowers with fixed-rate loans** (the real value of the debt falls), and the government as a debtor.
- **Other costs:** uncertainty (planning is hard, less investment), frequent repricing ("menu costs"), the cost of economising on cash.

So central banks aim not for zero but for **low and stable** inflation: the Hungarian National Bank (MNB) targets **3%** (with a ±1 percentage point tolerance band), the European Central Bank 2%.

## Disinflation, deflation, hyperinflation

- **Disinflation:** inflation **falls** but is still positive (prices rise more slowly).
- **Deflation:** the price level **falls**. It can be dangerous: buyers postpone purchases ("it will be cheaper next year"), demand falls, and the real burden of debt rises (Japan from the 1990s).
- **Hyperinflation:** inflation above 50% a month. The 1945–46 hyperinflation of the **Hungarian pengő** is the largest in world history: in July 1946 prices doubled about every 15 hours. The **forint** was introduced on 1 August 1946.

## Summary

- Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level; the CPI is the price of a weighted consumer basket.
- Core inflation: without volatile items; deflator: the price of all domestic output.
- Causes: demand, costs, expectations, money supply.
- It hurts people on fixed incomes and savers, and helps fixed-rate borrowers.
- Goal: low, stable inflation (MNB: 3%).
