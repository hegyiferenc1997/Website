## What you will learn

- the **causes** and **course** of the most important economic and financial crises,
- what **lessons** economic policy drew from them,
- how these crises affected **Hungary** and the **tech sector**.

## 1929–1933: the Great Depression

- **Trigger:** in October 1929 the New York stock market crashed after a credit-fuelled share bubble.
- **Deepening:** thousands of bank failures (there was no deposit insurance), the central bank did **not** replace liquidity, the money supply shrank, deflation; governments **tightened**, and protectionist tariffs (USA, 1930) cut back world trade.
- **Consequence:** US real GDP fell by a quarter, unemployment rose to around 25%; the crisis hit Europe, including Hungary, hard.
- **Lessons:** according to John Maynard Keynes, in a crisis the state must **stimulate demand**; the central bank must act as **lender of last resort**; **deposit insurance** was created (USA, 1933); tariff wars hurt everyone.

## 1973 and 1979: the oil crises and stagflation

- After the oil embargo of the OPEC countries the price of oil **quadrupled**, and another price surge came in 1979.
- **A negative supply shock:** recession and high inflation at the same time – **stagflation**.
- **Lesson:** stimulating demand does not solve a supply shock (it only raises inflation further); expectations matter (topic 8, Phillips curve). In the early 1980s the US Federal Reserve (Volcker) broke inflation with very high interest rates – central bank **independence** and **credibility** gained value.

## 2000–2002: the dot-com bubble

The most important crisis for a computer engineer:

- In the late 1990s the shares of internet companies skyrocketed – many companies had no profit, not even revenue, just a ".com" in their name and a bold business plan.
- The tech-heavy **Nasdaq index** peaked in March 2000 and fell about **78%** by autumn 2002. Countless start-ups went bankrupt.
- **But:** the fibre-optic cables, data centres and knowledge built up during the bubble laid the foundations of the later internet economy – Amazon and Google survived and became giants.
- **Lesson:** a technology can be revolutionary while company valuations are still excessive. Worth keeping in mind during today's **AI investment wave** too.

## 2007–2009: the global financial crisis

- **Background:** low interest rates in the USA, a housing bubble, loose lending (**subprime mortgages** to borrowers unable to repay). The loans were **securitised**, bundled and sold by banks all over the world – often with good ratings.
- **Outbreak:** house prices began to fall, borrowers stopped paying, and the value of the securities collapsed. On 15 September 2008 the investment bank **Lehman Brothers** went bankrupt – banks stopped trusting each other and the interbank market froze.
- **Global crisis:** in 2009 the world economy shrank for the first time since the Second World War.
- **Hungary:** because of high public debt and many **foreign-currency loans** we were especially vulnerable. In October 2008 the country received a loan package of about 20 billion euros in total from the **IMF, the EU and the World Bank**. In 2009 GDP fell by about 6–7%, and as the forint weakened, foreign-currency borrowers' instalments soared (topic 9).
- **Lessons:** stricter bank regulation (**Basel III**), macroprudential policy, tackling the "too big to fail" problem (lesson 3).

## 2010–2012: the euro area debt crisis

- It turned out that Greece's budget deficit was much larger than reported; markets lost confidence in several southern European countries too (Portugal, Ireland, Spain, Cyprus, Italy).
- Because of the common currency these countries **could not devalue** their money or decide on interest rates independently (topic 9).
- **Rescue packages** with strict austerity conditions; in Greece GDP fell by a quarter.
- In July 2012 ECB President Mario Draghi declared that the ECB would do **"whatever it takes"** to save the euro – this sentence alone calmed the markets.
- **Lesson:** a monetary union needs more common financial institutions (**banking union**, the European Stability Mechanism).

## 2020: the COVID-19 crisis

- A completely different type: the pandemic and the restrictions caused a **supply** shock (factories and supply chains shut down) and a **demand** shock (travel and hospitality collapsed) at the same time.
- **A fast and huge response:** wage subsidies (to keep jobs), loan moratoria (in Hungary from March 2020), central bank easing, bond purchases; the EU created the recovery fund (NextGenerationEU) through joint borrowing.
- The downturn was deep, but the recovery was also fast. **Digitalisation** took a giant leap: remote work, e-commerce, online education.

## 2021–2023: the energy and inflation crisis

- The rapid rise in demand after the pandemic, supply chain disruptions, and then the **energy price shock** following Russia's attack on Ukraine in 2022 brought inflation not seen for decades in Europe and the USA.
- In Hungary inflation rose above 25% in early 2023 (topic 6), and the MNB raised the base rate to 13% (topic 7).
- **Lesson:** low inflation cannot be taken for granted; energy dependence and fragile supply chains are macroeconomic risks.

## Summary

| Crisis  | Main cause                                               | Main lesson                                               |
| ------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1929    | stock market crash, bank failures, wrong economic policy | demand stimulus, lender of last resort, deposit insurance |
| 1970s   | oil price shock                                          | supply shock → stagflation; credible central bank         |
| 2000    | dot-com bubble                                           | revolutionary technology ≠ justified valuation            |
| 2008    | housing bubble, securitisation, excessive debt           | stricter bank regulation                                  |
| 2010–12 | euro debt crisis                                         | common institutions, "whatever it takes"                  |
| 2020    | pandemic                                                 | fast, large fiscal and monetary response                  |
| 2022    | energy price shock                                       | inflation can return                                      |
