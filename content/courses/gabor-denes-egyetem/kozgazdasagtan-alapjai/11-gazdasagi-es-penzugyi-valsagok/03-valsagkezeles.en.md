## What you will learn

- the **fiscal** and **monetary** tools of crisis management,
- how **banking crises** are handled (rescue, bail-in, deposit insurance),
- what **macroprudential** regulation is and how it **prevents** crises,
- the role of the **IMF**,
- what **moral hazard** is and why it must be taken into account.

## Three levels of crisis management

```text
 PREVENTION                    MANAGEMENT                  AFTERMATH
 regulation, buffers      →    stimulus, liquidity,   →    debt reduction,
 stress tests                  bank and firm rescues       structural reforms
```

## Fiscal tools

- **Automatic stabilisers** (topic 5): falling tax revenue and rising benefits dampen the downturn without any decision.
- **Discretionary stimulus:** infrastructure investment, tax cuts, direct support for households.
- **Targeted tools:** wage subsidies to keep jobs (during COVID), loan guarantees for firms, loan moratoria.

**Debate: austerity or stimulus?** In the euro crisis many countries tightened to reduce their debt – but the fall in demand also reduced GDP, so the debt **to GDP ratio** often did not improve. Today's consensus: do not tighten too early in a crisis, but build **buffers** in good times (low deficits, falling debt) so there is room for manoeuvre.

## Monetary tools

- **Interest rate cuts** (topic 7).
- **Lender of last resort:** liquidity for solvent but temporarily cash-short banks – by Walter Bagehot's (1873) rule "freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate".
- **Quantitative easing** (QE), if interest rates are already near zero.
- **Currency swap agreements** between central banks to deal with foreign currency shortages.
- **Communication:** a credible announcement ("whatever it takes") can stop a panic by itself.

## Handling banking crises

| Tool                       | Essence                                                                               | Advantage / disadvantage                          |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **bank rescue** (bail-out) | the state injects capital into the bank or takes over its bad assets                  | stops panic quickly / taxpayers pay, moral hazard |
| **bail-in**                | the bank's losses are borne first by shareholders and creditors (bondholders)         | spares taxpayers / complex, can cause fear        |
| **resolution**             | the authority takes over the bank and restructures or winds it down in an orderly way | important functions are preserved                 |
| **deposit insurance**      | deposits guaranteed up to 100,000 euros (topic 7)                                     | prevents bank runs                                |

After 2008 the EU created the **banking union**: the large banks of the euro area are supervised by the ECB, and there are common **resolution** rules (bail-in first, taxpayers' money only as a last resort).

## Prevention: macroprudential regulation

**Microprudential** supervision looks at the safety of individual banks, **macroprudential** policy at the stability of the **whole system**.

- **Capital requirements (Basel III):** banks must hold more and better-quality own capital to absorb losses.
- **Countercyclical capital buffer:** more capital must be set aside in good times, which can be used in a crisis.
- **Liquidity requirements:** enough assets that can quickly be turned into cash.
- **Stress tests:** what would happen to the bank in a severe (but plausible) crisis?
- **Debt brake rules:** since 2015 the MNB has limited how large a mortgage can be relative to the value of the property (**loan-to-value ratio**) and to the borrower's income (**payment-to-income ratio**) – so that the foreign-currency loan era is not repeated.
- **Extra capital requirements for systemically important banks** – to tackle the "too big to fail" problem.

## The role of the IMF

The **International Monetary Fund** (IMF, 1944, Bretton Woods) lends to countries in balance of payments crises – in return for economic policy **conditions** (e.g. deficit reduction, reforms). This is often controversial, because the conditions can be painful in the short run. Hungary used an IMF–EU loan in 2008 and repaid it early in 2013.

## Moral hazard

**Moral hazard:** if someone knows they will be rescued if things go wrong, they take **more risk**. If large banks are sure the state will bail them out, they lend more riskily – the profits are theirs, the losses the taxpayers'.

So crisis management must strike a balance: stop the panic, but in a way that makes those responsible bear the losses (bail-in, holding executives accountable, stricter rules).

## What can an entrepreneur or a household learn from this?

The same principles work on a small scale: **buffers** (an emergency fund, topic 5), **diversification** (several clients, several sources of income), **careful borrowing** (instalments relative to income), and a **stress test** ("what happens if I lose my biggest client?").

## Summary

- Fiscal: automatic stabilisers, stimulus, targeted support; buffers must be built in good times.
- Monetary: rate cuts, lender of last resort, QE, credible communication.
- Banking crisis: rescue, bail-in, resolution, deposit insurance; the EU banking union.
- Prevention: Basel III, capital buffers, stress tests, debt brake rules.
- IMF: loans with conditions; moral hazard: the promise of a rescue encourages risk-taking.
