## The business cycle

The economy does not grow smoothly: it **fluctuates** around its long-run growth trend.

```text
 real GDP
    │            peak                      peak
    │             ╱╲                        ╱
    │      ......╱..╲......trend........../.....
    │     ╱    ╱     ╲                   ╱
    │    ╱ ex-        ╲  con-          ╱
    │   ╱  pansion     ╲ traction    ╱
    │  ╱                ╲          ╱
    │                     ╲______╱
    │                       trough
    └──────────────────────────────────────────► time
```

| Phase                       | Feature                                                             |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **expansion**               | output, employment and investment rise                              |
| **peak**                    | the economy may overheat: high capacity use, accelerating inflation |
| **contraction** (recession) | output falls, unemployment rises                                    |
| **trough**                  | the end of the downturn, the new expansion starts here              |

- **Technical recession:** real GDP falls in two consecutive quarters.
- **Depression:** a very deep and long recession (e.g. the 1930s).
- **Output gap:** the difference between actual and **potential** GDP (achievable with normal use of the factors). Negative gap → recession, cyclical unemployment (topic 8, Okun); positive gap → overheating, inflationary pressure.

## Leading indicators

A turning point can be signalled in advance by:

- the **purchasing managers' index (PMI):** a survey of companies' purchasing managers; above 50 signals expansion, below 50 contraction,
- **consumer and business confidence indices,**
- **new orders**, building permits,
- **share prices,**
- **an inverted yield curve:** when short-term interest rates are higher than long-term ones, this often happens before a recession.

**Lagging** indicators (e.g. unemployment) only react afterwards.

## What causes the cycle? Shocks

- **Demand shock:** consumption, investment, government spending or exports change suddenly. E.g. a recession in a major export market, a loss of confidence.
- **Supply shock:** production costs or possibilities change suddenly. Negative: an oil price surge, supply chain disruption, a pandemic. Positive: a new technology, cheaper energy.

A negative supply shock is especially unpleasant: **output falls and inflation rises at the same time** (**stagflation**), putting economic policy in a dilemma.

## Bubbles and the financial cycle

**Bubble:** the price of an asset (shares, property, crypto assets) rises far above its "intrinsic value" because everyone expects further price rises.

According to Hyman Minsky, stability itself breeds instability:

1. **Initial enthusiasm:** a new technology or opportunity (the internet, cheap credit, AI).
2. **Boom and credit expansion:** prices rise, credit becomes ever easier to get.
3. **Euphoria:** "this time is different" – risks are underestimated, debt grows.
4. **Turning point:** something shakes confidence (a rate hike, a bankruptcy).
5. **Panic and crash:** everyone wants to sell at once, prices plunge, lending stops.

**Financial crises** are usually deeper and longer than "ordinary" recessions, because damage to the banking system also paralyses lending (topic 7), and indebted actors cut back their spending for years.

### Contagion

In today's globalised financial system the trouble of one country or one bank can quickly spread to others: banks owe each other, investors flee from everywhere, trade falls. That is why big crises become **global**.

## Summary

- Cycle: expansion → peak → contraction → trough.
- Technical recession: two quarters of decline; output gap: actual − potential GDP.
- Leading indicators: PMI, confidence indices, orders, the yield curve.
- Demand and supply shocks; a negative supply shock can cause stagflation.
- Bubbles and the Minsky cycle: enthusiasm → credit expansion → euphoria → panic.
