## What you will learn

- why labour demand is **derived**,
- how a firm decides on **headcount** (the value of the marginal product of labour),
- what shapes **labour supply**,
- how the **equilibrium wage** arises and what a **minimum wage** causes,
- why some people earn more than others.

## Labour demand

Labour demand is a **derived demand**: firms do not hire people for the sake of the work itself, but because there is demand for their products. If demand for AI applications rises, demand for machine learning engineers rises too.

### How many people should a firm hire?

- **Marginal product of labour (MP):** how much output increases from one more worker.
- **Marginal revenue product (MRP):** $MRP = MP \cdot P$ – how much extra revenue one more worker brings.

**Rule:** the firm hires another worker as long as **MRP ≥ wage**. (This is marginal analysis again – topics 1 and 4.)

**Example:** a web agency sells websites each month for 400,000 HUF each. The monthly wage is 900,000 HUF per developer.

| Developers | Websites finished / month | MP  | MRP (thousand HUF) |
| ---------- | ------------------------- | --- | ------------------ |
| 1          | 4                         | 4   | 1600               |
| 2          | 7                         | 3   | 1200               |
| 3          | 9                         | 2   | 800                |
| 4          | 10                        | 1   | 400                |

The 2nd developer's MRP (1,200,000 HUF) still exceeds the wage; the 3rd's (800,000 HUF) is already lower. **Optimum: 2 developers.** The marginal product falls because of **diminishing returns**.

So the demand curve **slopes downwards**: at a lower wage it is worth hiring more workers.

## Labour supply

Labour supply shows how many people want to work how many hours at different wages. The opportunity cost of work is **leisure**.

- **Substitution effect:** at a higher wage leisure is more expensive → we work more.
- **Income effect:** at a higher wage we are "richer" and can afford more leisure → we work less.

At low wages the substitution effect is usually stronger (supply rises with the wage); at very high wages the income effect can also be strong.

**Market** labour supply is also shaped by population, the retirement age, time spent in education, migration, childcare options and taxes.

## The equilibrium wage and the minimum wage

On a competitive labour market the wage settles where **labour demand = labour supply**.

The **minimum wage** is a **price floor** on the labour market (topic 3): if it is **above** the equilibrium wage, more labour is supplied than demanded – **unemployment** may arise, especially among less skilled and young workers.

In reality the effect is debated: many studies find that moderate minimum wage increases have small employment effects, because employers often have wage-setting power and a higher wage can reduce staff turnover. In Hungary, besides the minimum wage there is also a **guaranteed wage minimum** for jobs requiring at least secondary education.

## Why do wages differ?

- **Human capital:** knowledge and skills – more training, higher productivity, higher pay (lesson 3).
- **Compensating wage differentials:** dangerous, demanding, night-time or unpleasant work gets higher pay.
- **Demand and supply:** good cybersecurity specialists are scarce, so their pay is high.
- **Region:** wages are higher in Budapest than in the countryside (but so are prices).
- **Trade unions, collective agreements.**
- **Discrimination:** different pay for the same work because of gender or age – illegal, yet measurable (e.g. the gender pay gap).

## Summary

- Labour demand is derived; the firm hires as long as MRP ≥ wage.
- In labour supply the substitution and income effects compete.
- The equilibrium wage is where demand and supply meet; the minimum wage is a price floor.
- Wage differences: human capital, compensating differentials, demand and supply, region, discrimination.
