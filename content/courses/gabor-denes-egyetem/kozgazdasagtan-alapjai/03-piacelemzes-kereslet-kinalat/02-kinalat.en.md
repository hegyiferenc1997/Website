## What you will learn

- what **supply** and the **law of supply** are,
- what the **supply curve** looks like,
- which factors **shift** the supply curve,
- what **market supply** is.

## Supply

**Supply** shows how much of a product sellers **want and are able** to sell at different prices in a given period.

**The law of supply:** ceteris paribus, if the price of a product **rises**, the quantity supplied also **rises**. Why?

- at a higher price **profit is higher**, so producing more is worthwhile,
- firms with higher costs also **enter** the market,
- expanding production usually gets more and more expensive (increasing marginal cost – topic 4), which only a higher price covers.

## The supply curve

The supply curve **slopes upwards**. A linear example: $P = c + Q$, where **c** is the lowest price at which anyone supplies at all.

```plot

x: 0, 60
y: 0, 150
param: c = 10, 0, 60, 5
f: c + x # supply
dash: 10 + x # original
```

If **c** rises (e.g. raw materials become more expensive), the curve shifts **up/left**: less is supplied at every price.

## Movement along the curve or a shift?

As with demand:

- a change in the **own price** → movement along the supply curve (the **quantity supplied** changes),
- a change in **another factor** → the curve shifts (**supply** changes).

| Factor                                                                 | Effect on supply                      |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **factors of production** become more expensive (wages, energy, chips) | falls (shifts left)                   |
| better **technology**, automation                                      | rises (shifts right)                  |
| the **number of sellers** rises (new producers enter)                  | rises                                 |
| a **tax** on the product                                               | falls                                 |
| a **subsidy**                                                          | rises                                 |
| sellers **expect prices to rise**                                      | current supply falls (they hold back) |
| **natural disaster**, supply chain disruption                          | falls                                 |

**Example:** in 2020–2022 the chip shortage shifted the supply of car and graphics card makers to the left – fewer products, higher prices.

## Individual and market supply

**Market supply** is the **horizontal sum** of individual sellers' supply: at each price we add up how much each seller supplies.

| Price (HUF) | Firm A | Firm B | Market supply |
| ----------- | ------ | ------ | ------------- |
| 100         | 10     | 0      | 10            |
| 200         | 20     | 15     | 35            |
| 300         | 30     | 30     | 60            |

Market demand is obtained from individual demands in the same way.

## Elasticity of supply

Supply can also be elastic or inelastic. The supply of **software** is very elastic (an extra copy can be produced almost for free), the supply of **housing** is inelastic in the short run (building a house takes years), and the supply of **concert tickets** is perfectly inelastic (the venue's capacity is fixed).

## Summary

- Law of supply: higher price → higher quantity supplied.
- The supply curve slopes upwards.
- It is shifted by factor prices, technology, number of sellers, taxes/subsidies, expectations.
- Market supply = the sum of individual supplies at a given price.
