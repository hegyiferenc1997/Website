## What you will learn

- what **creative destruction** is,
- what **general purpose technologies** are and why their effects are slow,
- what the **productivity paradox** and the **Jevons paradox** are,
- how **artificial intelligence** may affect productivity, work and inequality,
- how the EU **regulates** artificial intelligence.

## Creative destruction

According to Joseph Schumpeter (1942), the engine of capitalism is **creative destruction**: new technologies and firms **displace** old ones.

- digital photography displaced film (Kodak's bankruptcy in 2012),
- streaming displaced DVD rental shops and music CDs,
- the smartphone displaced sat-navs, compact cameras and MP3 players.

The **destructive** side is painful (bankruptcies, layoffs, structural unemployment – topic 8); the **creative** side raises productivity and living standards in the long run (topic 10, TFP). The **incentive** for innovation is the hope of profit – which is why patent protection matters, but so does preventing monopolies from stifling competition.

## General purpose technologies

A few technologies transform **the whole economy**: the steam engine, electricity, the internal combustion engine, the computer and the internet – and probably **artificial intelligence**. These are **general purpose technologies**:

- they can be used in almost every industry,
- they keep improving and getting cheaper (e.g. **Moore's law**: the number of transistors on chips doubled roughly every two years),
- they trigger **complementary innovations** (electricity → the assembly line, household appliances; the internet → e-commerce, the cloud, platforms).

## The productivity paradox

Robert Solow in 1987: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics."

Explanations:

- **time lags:** the new technology requires reorganising firms, new skills and complementary investment – electricity also took decades before factories were transformed. The effect of computing showed up strongly in the USA in the second half of the 1990s,
- **measurement problems:** the value of free digital services (search, maps, Wikipedia) does not appear in GDP (topic 6),
- **redistribution:** part of the technology only reshuffles market share between firms.

The same question arises with AI today: experiments show significant productivity gains in some tasks (customer support, programming, writing), but when and to what extent this will show up in economy-wide productivity is still an open question.

## The Jevons paradox

In 1865 William Jevons observed that more efficient steam engines did **not reduce** but **increased** coal consumption: using coal became cheaper, so it started being used in many more places.

In the digital age: as computing power got cheaper, we use **far more** of it; making it cheaper to run an AI model may multiply AI use – and with it the **energy demand** of data centres. If demand is elastic enough (topic 3), efficiency gains bring more, not less, resource use.

## Artificial intelligence and work

Technology replaces **tasks** (topic 8). Three effects compete:

1. **Displacement effect:** the machine does some tasks more cheaply → demand for labour doing them falls.
2. **Productivity effect:** workers become more productive, products cheaper, demand rises → demand for labour in other tasks may rise.
3. **New tasks:** new jobs and industries appear (before the internet there were no social media managers or cloud architects).

The outcome depends on how far technology **complements** and how far it **substitutes** for human work. Unlike earlier automation, AI also affects **cognitive**, white-collar tasks – including part of higher-skilled jobs.

### Inequality

- Technology may raise the value of those with **complementary** skills and lower the value of substitutable routine work (**skill-biased technological change**).
- In winner-takes-all markets (lesson 1) profits may concentrate in a few firms.
- **Digital divide:** those without access to technology and knowledge (across regions, age groups, countries) may fall behind.

Responses: education and **lifelong learning**, retraining, competition policy, modernising the social safety net.

## Regulating AI

The EU **Artificial Intelligence Act** (AI Act, 2024) is the world's first comprehensive AI regulation, applying in stages:

- a **risk-based** approach: a **ban** on applications with unacceptable risk (e.g. social scoring), strict requirements for **high-risk** systems (e.g. recruitment, credit scoring, critical infrastructure), **transparency** obligations for limited risk (e.g. people must be told when they are talking to a chatbot or seeing AI-generated content),
- separate rules for **general-purpose AI models**.

From an economic point of view the question is always the same: how can **externalities** and **risks** be handled without stifling innovation?

## Summary

- Creative destruction: the new displaces the old – painful, but it raises productivity in the long run.
- General purpose technologies transform the whole economy, but with a lag.
- Productivity paradox: the effect is delayed and hard to measure; Jevons paradox: efficiency can bring more use.
- AI and work: displacement, productivity and new-task effects; risks for inequality.
- EU AI Act: risk-based regulation.
