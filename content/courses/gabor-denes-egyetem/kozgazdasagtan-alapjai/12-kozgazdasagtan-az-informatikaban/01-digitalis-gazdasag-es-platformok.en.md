## What you will learn

- how **information goods** differ from traditional products,
- what **network effects** are and why they lead to **winner-takes-all** markets,
- how **two-sided platforms** work,
- what **lock-in** and **switching costs** are,
- why **data** and **attention** are valuable, and how large platforms are **regulated**.

## Information goods

Software, music, films and e-books are **information goods**. Their special features:

- **High fixed cost, almost zero marginal cost** (topic 4): the first copy costs millions, the rest can be copied almost for free → huge economies of scale.
- **Non-rival consumption:** if you use it, I can still use it just as much (as with public goods – topic 1).
- **Excludability only through technical and legal means** (licences, copy protection, copyright) – otherwise nobody would pay because of free riding.
- **Experience goods:** you only know what it is worth after trying it – that is why free trials and freemium spread (lesson 2).

## Network effects

**Network effect** (network externality): a product is more valuable the more people use it.

- **Direct:** a messaging app is only good if your friends use it too.
- **Indirect:** an operating system is valuable because many applications are made for it – and many applications are made for it because many people use it.

**Metcalfe's law:** in a network with $n$ users the number of possible connections is $\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$, so the value of the network grows **faster** than the number of users. With 10 users there are 45 possible connections, with 100 already 4,950.

### Winner takes all

Network effects and economies of scale together create **positive feedback**: whoever gets ahead attracts more and more users, and the others fall behind. That is why digital markets typically have a few dominant players (search engines, social media, mobile operating systems, cloud providers) – **oligopoly** or near **monopoly** (topic 4).

Before reaching **critical mass**, however, things are hard: an empty new social network is not attractive. That is why start-ups often launch at a loss, for free or at big discounts.

## Two-sided platforms

A **platform** connects two (or more) groups of users, and each side values it more the more people there are on the other side:

| Platform                    | One side   | Other side  |
| --------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| app store                   | users      | developers  |
| ride-hailing app            | passengers | drivers     |
| online marketplace          | buyers     | sellers     |
| search engine, social media | users      | advertisers |

The platform often serves one side **for free** (or subsidises it) and collects revenue from the other – users search for free, advertisers pay.

## Lock-in and switching costs

**Switching cost:** what it costs to change provider – migrating data, learning a new system, losing compatibility, existing connections. If it is high, the user is **locked in**.

- Examples: a company's entire infrastructure is built on one cloud provider's proprietary services; your photos are in a particular ecosystem.
- The provider may exploit this (price rises), which is why **open standards** and **data portability** matter (under the GDPR you have the right to your own data in a machine-readable format).

## Data and attention

- **Data** has become a factor of production: it improves services (recommender systems), machine learning models, and enables targeted advertising. Data also has a **network effect**: more users → more data → a better product → more users.
- **Attention economy:** with "free" services the user pays with their attention and data – if you are not paying for the product, you are often **the product**.
- This can cause externalities: invasion of privacy, addictive design, the spread of misinformation.

## Regulating platforms

Because of market power and externalities, the EU has created specific rules:

- **GDPR** (2018): data protection, consent, data portability.
- **Digital Markets Act (DMA)**: the largest "gatekeeper" platforms may not, for example, favour their own services, and must allow other app stores and services.
- **Digital Services Act (DSA)**: handling illegal content, transparency of algorithms.
- The traditional tools of **competition law** (topic 4): fines for abuse of market power, merger reviews.

## Summary

- Information goods: high fixed, almost zero marginal cost, non-rival, experience goods.
- Network effects: more users → a more valuable product; winner takes all.
- Two-sided platforms connect two groups; one side is often free.
- Lock-in: high switching costs; the remedy is open standards and data portability.
- Data is a factor of production; large platforms are regulated by the GDPR, the DMA and the DSA.
