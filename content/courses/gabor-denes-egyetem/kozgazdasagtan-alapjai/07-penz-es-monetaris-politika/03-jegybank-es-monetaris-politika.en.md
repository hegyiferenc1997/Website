## What you will learn

- what the central bank's **primary objective** is and why it is **independent**,
- how the **policy rate** affects the economy (**transmission**),
- when the central bank **tightens** and when it **eases**,
- what **inflation targeting** is,
- what unconventional tools such as **quantitative easing** are.

## The central bank's goals

By law the primary objective of the **Hungarian National Bank** (MNB) is to achieve and maintain **price stability**. Without endangering this, it supports the stability of the financial system and the government's economic policy. In the euro area the **European Central Bank** (ECB) does the same.

The MNB pursues **inflation targeting**: the target is **3%** inflation with a **±1 percentage point** tolerance band. The target is public, so it also anchors expectations.

### Why is the central bank independent?

A government facing an election would be tempted to "print money" or keep interest rates low – a boom in the short run, inflation later. An **independent** central bank can think over a longer horizon, so it is more **credible**, and a credible central bank can break inflation expectations with smaller rate hikes. EU rules also prohibit direct central bank financing of the state.

## The tools

| Tool                                 | Essence                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **policy rate** (base rate)          | the main rate at which banks can place money with the central bank; the main "switch" of monetary policy |
| **interest rate corridor**           | the central bank's overnight deposit and lending rates; they keep interbank rates between them           |
| **open market operations**           | buying/selling securities to regulate liquidity                                                          |
| **reserve requirement**              | banks must hold part of their deposits at the central bank                                               |
| **communication** (forward guidance) | signalling the future rate path to shape expectations                                                    |

## Monetary transmission

How does a rate hike reach inflation? Through several channels, **with a lag** (it typically takes 1–2 years to have its full effect):

```text
 policy rate ↑
   │
   ├─► interbank and lending rates ↑ ──► less credit, investment, home buying
   ├─► deposit rates ↑ ───────────────► saving pays more, less consumption
   ├─► forint strengthens ────────────► cheaper imports, less imported inflation
   └─► expectations: "the central bank means it" ──► smaller price and wage rises
                     │
                     ▼
            demand ↓  →  inflation ↓
```

## Tightening and easing

|             | Tightening (contractionary)                   | Easing (expansionary)      |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| when?       | high inflation, overheating economy           | low inflation, recession   |
| policy rate | raise                                         | cut                        |
| effect      | demand and inflation slow, but so does growth | lending and demand pick up |

**Hungarian example:** to break the 2022–2023 inflation, the MNB raised the base rate to **13%** by autumn 2022 (by far the highest in the EU), then gradually lowered it from 2023 as inflation fell.

## Unconventional tools

If the interest rate is already **near zero** but the economy is still weak (after 2008, in 2020), central banks turn to other tools:

- **Quantitative easing (QE):** the central bank buys government bonds or other securities on a large scale, lowering long-term interest rates and putting money into the economy.
- **Targeted lending programmes:** cheap central bank funding for banks if they lend to SMEs (in Hungary, e.g. the Funding for Growth Scheme).
- **Negative interest rates:** the ECB applied a negative deposit rate between 2014 and 2022.

## Monetary and fiscal policy

|       | Monetary                     | Fiscal (topic 5)                |
| ----- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| who?  | central bank                 | government, parliament          |
| tool  | interest rates, liquidity    | taxes, spending                 |
| speed | quick to decide, slow to act | slow to decide, targeted effect |

The two are most effective when they **pull in the same direction**. If the government spends heavily while the central bank wants to break inflation, the central bank needs higher interest rates.

## Summary

- The MNB's primary objective is price stability: 3% ± 1 percentage point.
- An independent central bank is more credible.
- The main tool is the policy rate; its effect arrives via the credit, saving, exchange rate and expectations channels, with a lag.
- Against inflation: rate hikes (tightening); in recession: rate cuts (easing).
- Near zero rates: quantitative easing, targeted lending programmes.
