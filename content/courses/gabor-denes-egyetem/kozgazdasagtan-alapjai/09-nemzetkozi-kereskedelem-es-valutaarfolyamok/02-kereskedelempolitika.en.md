## What you will learn

- the **tools** of trade policy (tariffs, quotas, non-tariff barriers, subsidies),
- the **effect** of a tariff on the market,
- the arguments for and against **protectionism**,
- the stages of **economic integration** and the **EU single market**,
- the role of the **WTO**.

## Tools of trade policy

| Tool                    | Essence                               | Example                                                            |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **tariff**              | a tax on an imported product          | EU tariffs on Chinese electric cars (2024)                         |
| **quota**               | a limit on the quantity of imports    | import quotas for agricultural products                            |
| **non-tariff barriers** | standards, licences, red tape         | different technical regulations                                    |
| **export subsidy**      | helping domestic exporters            | subsidised export credit                                           |
| **export restriction**  | banning exports of strategic products | export controls on the most advanced chips and chipmaking machines |

## The effect of a tariff

A tariff on an imported product (topic 3, tax):

- the product becomes **more expensive** on the domestic market,
- **domestic producers** can sell more at a higher price – they **gain**,
- the **government** collects tariff revenue,
- **consumers** buy less at a higher price – they **lose**,
- consumers' loss is **greater** than producers' gain and tariff revenue combined → **deadweight loss**.

What's more, other countries may **retaliate** with their own tariffs – so domestic exporters lose too.

## Arguments for and against protectionism

| Argument for                                                                       | Counter-argument                                                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| protecting **jobs**                                                                | consumers and manufacturing get products more expensively; retaliation threatens jobs in exports |
| **infant industry**: a young industry needs time to become competitive             | protection rarely ends, and the protected industry does not become efficient                     |
| **national security**: strategic products (chips, medicines, energy)               | may be justified, but is also open to abuse                                                      |
| **unfair competition**: dumping, state subsidies                                   | the WTO has procedures for this                                                                  |
| **security of supply**: the pandemic and wars showed how fragile supply chains are | diversification (more suppliers) can be cheaper than isolation                                   |

In the 2020s **"strategic autonomy"** has become increasingly important: both the EU and the USA support domestic chip manufacturing (e.g. the EU Chips Act, 2023).

## Stages of economic integration

1. **Free trade area:** no tariffs between members, but each sets its own external tariffs.
2. **Customs union:** + a **common external tariff** (the EU has been a customs union since 1968).
3. **Common (single) market:** + the **four freedoms**: free movement of goods, services, capital and labour (the EU single market since 1993).
4. **Economic and monetary union:** + a **common currency** and common monetary policy (the euro area, since 1999).

Hungary has been an EU member since 2004 – it is part of the single market, but not of the euro area. Roughly three quarters of Hungarian exports go to EU countries, and the largest partner is Germany.

## The WTO

The **World Trade Organization** (WTO, 1995) aims to liberalise and regulate trade:

- members cut tariffs through negotiations,
- **most-favoured-nation principle:** what you grant one member you must grant all others,
- a **dispute settlement procedure** for trade conflicts.

In recent years the WTO's role has weakened; the USA began broad tariff increases from 2018 and again in 2025, to which several partners responded with counter-tariffs. **Trade wars** generally hurt both sides.

## Summary

- Tools: tariffs, quotas, non-tariff barriers, export subsidies or restrictions.
- A tariff benefits domestic producers and the government and hurts consumers; overall there is a deadweight loss.
- Protectionist arguments: jobs, infant industry, national security – but there are strong counter-arguments.
- Integration: free trade area → customs union → single market → monetary union.
- The WTO guards trade rules, with the most-favoured-nation principle.
