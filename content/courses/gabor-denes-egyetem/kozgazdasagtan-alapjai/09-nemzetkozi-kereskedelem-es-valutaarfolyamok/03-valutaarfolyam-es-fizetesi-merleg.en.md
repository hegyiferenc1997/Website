## What you will learn

- how to **read** an exchange rate (EUR/HUF),
- what **appreciation** and **depreciation** mean, and whom each one benefits,
- what **moves** the exchange rate,
- what **exchange rate regimes** exist, and the pros and cons of the **euro**,
- what the **balance of payments** shows.

## The exchange rate

The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. It is set on the foreign exchange market by supply and demand.

**EUR/HUF = 400** means: **1 euro = 400 forints**.

- If EUR/HUF **rises to 420**, you need more forints for 1 euro → the forint has **weakened** (depreciated), the euro has strengthened.
- If it **falls to 380**, the forint has **strengthened** (appreciated).

Note: "the exchange rate rises" here means the forint **weakens** – a common misunderstanding.

**Conversion:** a 1,500-euro laptop costs $1500 \cdot 400 = 600\,000$ HUF at a rate of 400, and $630\,000$ HUF at 420.

## Whom does a strong or a weak forint benefit?

|                                                                      | Weakening forint                                       | Strengthening forint |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- |
| **exporters** (e.g. a Hungarian software company invoicing in euros) | **gain**: the same euros are worth more forints        | lose                 |
| **importers, consumers** (imported laptop, holiday abroad)           | lose: imports become more expensive                    | **gain**             |
| **inflation**                                                        | **rises** (more expensive imports, imported inflation) | falls                |
| **foreign-currency borrowers**                                       | lose (repayments cost more forints)                    | gain                 |

The Hungarian lesson of **foreign-currency loans**: in the 2000s many people took out mortgages in Swiss francs because of lower interest rates; when the forint weakened after 2008, instalments soared. In 2014–2015 these loans were converted into forints.

## What moves the exchange rate?

- **Interest rate differential:** if the MNB raises rates, forint investments become more attractive and demand for the forint rises → it **strengthens** (topic 7, transmission).
- **Inflation:** higher domestic inflation weakens the currency in the longer run.
- **Trade:** more exports → more euros flow in and are converted into forints → it strengthens.
- **Investor sentiment, risk:** in a crisis investors flee to "safe" currencies (dollar, Swiss franc), and smaller currencies weaken.
- **Expectations** and the predictability of politics and economic policy.

### Purchasing power parity (PPP)

In the long run the exchange rate tends towards a level at which the same basket of goods costs the same everywhere. If a basket costs 100 euros in Germany and 36,000 HUF in Hungary, the PPP exchange rate is 360 HUF/euro. The market rate can deviate from this for a long time (non-traded services – haircuts, rent – are cheaper in poorer countries).

## Exchange rate regimes

| Regime              | Essence                                                                             | Example                                        |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **floating**        | set by the market, the central bank intervenes rarely if at all                     | forint (since 2008), dollar, euro              |
| **fixed**           | the central bank pegs it to another currency and defends this with foreign reserves | the Danish krone is closely pegged to the euro |
| **common currency** | no own money and no own monetary policy                                             | the euro area                                  |

### Pros and cons of the euro

- **Pros:** no conversion costs or exchange rate risk, easier price comparison, deeper capital markets, possibly lower interest rates.
- **Cons:** no independent interest rate policy or exchange rate – a country cannot respond to its own crisis with interest rates or devaluation (the Greek crisis, topic 11).
- The conditions for joining are the **Maastricht criteria** (inflation, interest rates, deficit, debt, exchange rate stability). Hungary has committed to adopting the euro but has not set a date; in 2026 **21 countries** use the euro (Bulgaria joined on 1 January 2026).

## The balance of payments

The **balance of payments** records all economic transactions between a country and the rest of the world over a period.

- **Current account:** exports and imports of goods and services (the **trade balance**), income (wages, dividends, interest) and transfers (e.g. part of EU funds, remittances).
- **Capital and financial account:** investments and loans – **foreign direct investment** (e.g. a foreign company builds a factory), portfolio investment (shares, government bonds), loans.

If the current account is in **deficit** (we import more than we export), it must be financed by **inflows** on the financial account (foreign loans or investment). A persistently large deficit makes a country vulnerable.

## Summary

- EUR/HUF = 400: 1 euro = 400 HUF; if it rises, the forint weakens.
- A weak forint is good for exporters, bad for importers, and raises inflation.
- The exchange rate is moved by interest rate differentials, inflation, trade, risk and expectations.
- Exchange rate regimes: floating, fixed, common currency.
- Balance of payments: current account + capital and financial account.
