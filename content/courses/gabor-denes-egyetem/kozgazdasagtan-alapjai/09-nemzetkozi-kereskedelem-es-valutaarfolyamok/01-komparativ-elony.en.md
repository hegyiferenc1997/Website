## What you will learn

- what **absolute** and **comparative advantage** are,
- how to calculate comparative advantage from **opportunity cost**,
- why both sides gain from **specialisation** and trade,
- who the **losers** from trade can be.

## Why do countries trade?

- they have different **resources** (oil, farmland, skilled labour),
- a larger market allows **economies of scale** (topic 4),
- consumers get **more variety**,
- and – the deepest reason – **comparative advantage**.

## Absolute advantage

A country has an **absolute advantage** in a product if it can produce it with **fewer resources** (e.g. labour hours) than the other. According to Adam Smith, everyone should specialise in what they have an absolute advantage in.

But what if one country is better at **everything**?

## Comparative advantage (David Ricardo, 1817)

A country has a **comparative advantage** in a product if it produces it at a **lower opportunity cost** – that is, it has to give up less of the other product for it.

**Example:** labour hours needed to produce one unit.

|             | Software package | Car      |
| ----------- | ---------------- | -------- |
| **Hungary** | 10 hours         | 40 hours |
| **Germany** | 5 hours          | 10 hours |

Germany has an **absolute advantage in both**. Let us calculate the opportunity costs:

|             | Price of 1 software in cars | Price of 1 car in software |
| ----------- | --------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| **Hungary** | 10/40 = **0.25** cars       | 40/10 = 4 software         |
| **Germany** | 5/10 = 0.5 cars             | 10/5 = **2** software      |

- **Software** is cheaper in Hungary (0.25 < 0.5 cars) → **Hungary's comparative advantage is software**.
- **Cars** are cheaper in Germany (2 < 4 software) → **Germany's comparative advantage is cars**.

## The gains from trade

If Hungary specialises in software and Germany in cars, and the exchange ratio lies **between** the two domestic ratios (e.g. 1 car = 3 software), both gain:

- Hungary gives 3 software for 1 car, whereas at home it would cost 4 software → **it gains 1 software**.
- Germany receives 3 software for 1 car, whereas at home it would be worth only 2 software → **it gains 1 software**.

Together the two countries produce **more** with the same labour than if everyone made everything themselves. Consumption outside the production possibility frontier (topic 1) becomes attainable.

> The same is true in teams: even the best senior developer is better off leaving documentation to a junior, because the opportunity cost of their time is greatest when writing documentation.

## There are losers too

Trade benefits **the country as a whole**, but not everyone:

- workers in industries that become uncompetitive may lose their jobs (structural unemployment – topic 8),
- some regions may fall behind.

That is why **retraining** and **redistributing** part of the winners' gains to the losers matter. Anti-trade sentiment (protectionism) often feeds on these losses.

## Summary

- Absolute advantage: fewer resources needed for production.
- Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost – this decides what to specialise in.
- With specialisation and exchange both sides can gain, even if one is better at everything.
- Trade has losers, so retraining and redistribution are needed.
