## What you will learn

- Paul Watzlawick's **five axioms**,
- the difference between the **content** and **relationship** levels,
- what **punctuation** means in an argument,
- how to use all this in a team.

## The five axioms

In the 1960s Paul Watzlawick and his colleagues summarised how human communication works in five axioms.

### 1. One cannot not communicate

All behaviour is a message. If someone does not reply to a chat message, or stares at their phone throughout a meeting, that says something too – just not necessarily what they would like.

### 2. Every communication has a content and a relationship level

- **Content level**: _what_ we say (the information).
- **Relationship level**: _how_ we say it, and what that reveals about our relationship.

"Have you looked at my pull request yet?" – someone can say this curiously, impatiently or reproachfully. The content is the same, the relationship message is completely different. **The relationship level determines how we interpret the content.**

### 3. Punctuation of communication

Each party "segments" an ongoing interaction differently: everyone sees themselves as merely _reacting_ to the other.

```text
Tester:    "I report bugs sharply because the developer doesn't fix them."
Developer: "I don't hurry with fixes because the tester always attacks."
```

Both statements are true from their own viewpoint – the solution is not asking "who started it" but breaking the cycle.

### 4. Digital and analogue communication

- **Digital** (here: verbal, linguistic): precise, logical, good at conveying content.
- **Analogue** (nonverbal: tone, facial expression, gesture): expressive, mainly conveys the relationship.

A written chat message lacks the analogue part, so it is easier to misread – emojis partly make up for this.

### 5. Symmetrical and complementary relationships

- **Symmetrical**: the parties behave as equals (two senior developers debating).
- **Complementary**: their behaviour complements each other, one leads and the other follows (mentor and intern).

Neither is good or bad in itself; the problem arises when the parties see their relationship differently.

## What to do with it in practice

- If a conversation gets stuck, check whether the problem is on the **relationship level** ("I feel you don't trust my code").
- In writing, watch your tone, because the nonverbal part is missing.
- In an argument, don't look for an answer to "who started it".

## Summary

- One cannot not communicate: silence is also a message.
- Every message has a content and a relationship level; the relationship level frames the content.
- Parties punctuate events differently.
- Communication is both digital (verbal) and analogue (nonverbal).
- A relationship can be symmetrical or complementary.
