## What you will learn

- how **recommendation algorithms** work and what a **filter bubble** is,
- how to **check** a news story or claim,
- how to recognise **phishing** and **social engineering**,
- how to protect your **accounts**.

## Algorithms and filter bubbles

The **recommendation algorithms** of social platforms show what is most likely to **hold** your attention (clicks, shares, watch time). The consequences:

- **Filter bubble**: you increasingly see only content that confirms your existing views.
- **Echo chamber**: like-minded people reinforce each other and crowd out other opinions.
- **Emotional**, outrageous content spreads faster than accurate but duller information.

As a computer engineer it is especially worth understanding this – one day you might build such a system.

## Recognising misinformation

Before sharing something, ask:

1. **Who is the source?** Known, reliable? Is there an author and a date?
2. **What do other sources say?** If only one site carries a sensation, be suspicious.
3. **When was it made?** An old story may be shared as new.
4. **Does the headline match the content?** Clickbait headlines often exaggerate.
5. **Is the image or video real?** A reverse image search shows where it came from; it might be AI-generated or manipulated (deepfake).
6. **What feeling does it evoke?** If it outrages or scares you a lot, stop – that's the goal.

## Phishing

**Phishing** is a deceptive message (email, SMS, chat) that tries to get you to enter a password, card details or other sensitive data, or to download malware.

Suspicious signs:

- **urgency or threats**: "Your account will be deleted within 24 hours!",
- **odd sender**: `support@neptun-login-hu.co` instead of the official domain,
- the link **points somewhere else** than it shows (hover over it!),
- an **unexpected attachment** (e.g. "invoice.zip", ".exe"),
- they **ask for** a password or code – an official provider never asks for your password.

## Social engineering

**Social engineering** (psychological manipulation) attacks the person, not the machine. The attacker builds trust, or plays on authority, urgency or helpfulness (see Cialdini's principles in topic 5).

Examples:

- an "IT person" calls and asks for your password "for maintenance",
- someone in a colleague's name urgently asks for a money transfer in chat,
- a stranger follows you through the access gate ("I forgot my card").

Defence: **verify through another channel** (call the known number), and never give your password to anyone.

## Protecting your accounts

- A **strong, unique password** for every service – use a **password manager**.
- Turn on **two-factor authentication** (2FA), preferably with an authenticator app or a security key.
- Review your **privacy settings**: who sees your posts, your phone number, your location?
- Don't share **too much** (home address, travel dates, photos of ID documents).
- Be careful with sensitive matters on public Wi-Fi.

## Summary

- The algorithm optimises for your attention – which can lead to a filter bubble.
- Checking misinformation: source, other sources, date, headline vs. content, image, emotions.
- Phishing: urgency, odd sender, deceptive link, unexpected attachment, password request.
- Social engineering: verify through another channel; your password belongs to nobody else.
- Strong, unique passwords, a password manager, 2FA, considered privacy settings.
