## What you will learn

- where to find **reliable literature**,
- how to **evaluate** a source's credibility,
- how to **cite** (APA, IEEE),
- what **plagiarism** is and how to avoid it.

## Where to search?

| Source                                                | Features                                                    |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Google Scholar**                                    | scientific papers, citation counts, free search             |
| **IEEE Xplore**, **ACM Digital Library**              | the main databases of engineering and computing literature  |
| **arXiv**                                             | preprints (not yet peer-reviewed) – use with care           |
| **university library**, database subscriptions        | many paid papers are accessible from the university network |
| **official documentation**, standards (e.g. RFC, ISO) | the primary source for technical questions                  |

Search tips: English keywords, phrases in quotes, synonyms; the references of a good, recent overview paper (_survey_, _review_) lead further.

## Source evaluation

Not every source is equally reliable. Ask (based on the "CRAAP" test):

- **Current?** When was it published? In computing a 10-year-old paper may be outdated.
- **Relevant?** Is it really about your question?
- **Authority?** Who wrote it, with what expertise, from what institution?
- **Accurate?** Does it have a methodology, references, data? Is it **peer-reviewed** (checked by other experts before publication)?
- **Purpose?** Does it inform, or want to sell something?

| More reliable                              | Handle with care                                     |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| peer-reviewed journal or conference papers | anonymous blogs, forum posts                         |
| official documentation, standards          | a company's marketing about its own product          |
| textbooks, professional books              | social media, an AI chatbot's answer without sources |

Wikipedia is a good **starting point** for orientation and finding further sources, but in academic work you should generally cite the original sources.

## Citation

You must cite every **borrowed idea, data, figure or quote** – even if you rephrased it in your own words.

### APA style (author–date)

In the text: "…practice testing is among the most effective techniques (Dunlosky et al., 2013)."

In the reference list:

```text
Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013).
Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. Psychological Science
in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58.
```

### IEEE style (numbered) – common in engineering literature

In the text: "…practice testing is among the most effective techniques [1]."

```text
[1] J. Dunlosky, K. A. Rawson, E. J. Marsh, M. J. Nathan, and D. T. Willingham,
    "Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques," Psychological
    Science in the Public Interest, vol. 14, no. 1, pp. 4–58, 2013.
```

Use a **reference manager** (e.g. Zotero) that formats citations automatically.

## Plagiarism

**Plagiarism**: presenting someone else's work (text, ideas, code, figures) as your own without proper citation. Its forms:

- verbatim copying without quotation marks and citation,
- paraphrasing without citation,
- submitting someone else's code as your own,
- reusing your own earlier work without disclosure (self-plagiarism).

How to avoid it?

- When taking notes, **always write down the source**.
- Verbatim quotes go **in quotation marks**, with a citation.
- Cite paraphrases too.
- For code, respect the **licence** and mark where a borrowed snippet comes from.

## Artificial intelligence in research

- An AI chatbot is **not a source**: it can invent non-existent papers and references. Check every claim in the original source.
- If you used an AI tool (e.g. for editing text, brainstorming), **disclose** it according to the rules of the institution or journal.
- Don't upload confidential research data (e.g. interview transcripts) to a public AI service without permission.

## Summary

- Search reliable databases (Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL, university library).
- Source evaluation: current, relevant, authoritative author, accurate, peer-reviewed, purpose.
- Cite every borrowed idea – in APA (author–date) or IEEE (numbered) style.
- Plagiarism: others' work without citation – avoid it with quotation marks, citations, licences.
- AI is not a source; verify it and disclose its use.
