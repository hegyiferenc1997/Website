## What you will learn

- where **conflicts** come from,
- why a conflict can be **useful**,
- the five styles of the **Thomas–Kilmann model**,
- the **steps** of a difficult conversation.

## What is conflict?

**Conflict** exists when two parties' **goals, interests, values or views clash**, and both perceive it.

Common sources in a team:

- **resources**: little time, money, people ("who gets the senior developer?"),
- **goals**: different priorities (speed vs. quality),
- **roles**: unclear who is responsible for what,
- **information**: the parties know different things,
- **values, personality**: different working and communication styles.

## Conflict isn't always bad

- **Task conflict** (what we argue about: solutions, plans) – at a moderate level it **improves** decisions, because more perspectives come up.
- **Relationship conflict** (personal animosity, hurt feelings) – almost always **harms** the team.

The goal is not to avoid conflict, but to keep task conflict from turning into relationship conflict.

## The Thomas–Kilmann model

Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann placed the styles on two axes:

- **assertiveness**: how much you try to satisfy **your own** interests,
- **cooperativeness**: how much you try to satisfy the **other's** interests.

```text
 assertiveness
     ▲
 high │  COMPETING             COLLABORATING
     │              COMPROMISING
     │
 low  │  AVOIDING              ACCOMMODATING
     └──────────────────────────────────────► cooperativeness
          low                      high
```

| Style             | Essence                 | When is it good?                                                       | Risk                         |
| ----------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| **competing**     | I win, you lose         | emergency (production outage), security issue, quick decision          | resentful colleagues         |
| **accommodating** | you win                 | when it matters less to you, or you were wrong; building relationships | being exploited, frustration |
| **avoiding**      | nobody wins (for now)   | trivial matter; when people need to calm down; not enough information  | the problem grows            |
| **compromising**  | everyone gives a little | time pressure, equal parties, temporary solution                       | nobody is really satisfied   |
| **collaborating** | we both win             | important issue, long-term relationship, there is time                 | time- and energy-consuming   |

> There's no always-"best" style – choose for the situation. The problem is when someone **always uses the same one**.

## Steps of a difficult conversation

1. **Timing and place**: one-on-one, at a calm moment, not in front of the team and not in chat.
2. Describe the **facts** without judging (I-message, see topic 3).
3. **Listen** to the other's point of view – with active listening.
4. State a **shared goal**: "We both want…"
5. Look for **solutions** together (Harvard method).
6. **Agree** on concrete steps, and **follow up** later: "Let's check in two weeks whether it works."

If the parties can't settle it alone, a neutral third party (a **mediator**, e.g. the team lead) can help.

## Summary

- Conflict is a clash of interests, goals or values; sources: resources, goals, roles, information, personality.
- Task conflict can be useful; relationship conflict is harmful.
- Thomas–Kilmann: competing, accommodating, avoiding, compromising, collaborating – adapt to the situation.
- Difficult conversation: one-on-one, facts, listening, shared goal, joint solution, follow-up.
