## What you will learn

- the difference between **position-based** and **interest-based** negotiation,
- the **four principles** of the Harvard method,
- how to apply it in a dispute within a team.

## Position or interest?

A **position** is what someone **demands**. An **interest** is **why** they demand it.

The classic example: two people argue over an orange. Both want the whole thing (position). If we ask, it turns out one needs the juice and the other the peel for a cake (interest). Instead of cutting it in half, both can get their full share.

| Position                        | Possible interest                               |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| "I can't deliver it on Friday." | too many parallel tasks; missing information    |
| "I insist on full remote work." | commuting time; concentration; family situation |
| "We only support the old API."  | no capacity for migration; fear of bugs         |

> Ask: "What matters to you in this?" or "What would happen if…?" – that's how you get to the interest behind the position.

## The four principles (Fisher–Ury: _Getting to Yes_)

### 1. Separate the people from the problem

Be **hard on the problem** and **soft on the people**. The other party is not an enemy but a partner in solving a shared problem. Acknowledge emotions ("I understand this is frustrating"), but don't mix them with the merits of the case.

### 2. Focus on interests, not positions

Positions often exclude each other, but interests are often **compatible**.

### 3. Invent options for mutual gain

Before deciding, **collect several options** – don't criticise while brainstorming. Look for things that are cheap for you and valuable for the other side.

### 4. Insist on objective criteria

If interests clash, let the decision depend not on who is more stubborn but on an **external, fair standard**: market price, industry standard, measurement data, expert opinion, past practice.

## Example: a deadline dispute in the team

> The product manager wants the whole new payment flow live in two weeks. The development team says it will take at least six weeks.

- **People and problem**: "We both want the release to succeed."
- **Interests**: the product manager needs _something_ working in two weeks for a partner company's demo; the team fears that rushing leads to faulty payments.
- **Options**: a demo in the test environment in two weeks; launching a single payment method; the full flow in six weeks, in stages.
- **Objective criteria**: the actual development time of similar past features; the payment provider's mandatory testing steps.

Result: a demo + one payment method in two weeks, the full flow in six weeks. Both parties' interests are met.

## Summary

- Position: what we demand; interest: why.
- Harvard principles: people ≠ problem; interests, not positions; mutual gain; objective criteria.
- Ask about interests and collect several options before deciding.
