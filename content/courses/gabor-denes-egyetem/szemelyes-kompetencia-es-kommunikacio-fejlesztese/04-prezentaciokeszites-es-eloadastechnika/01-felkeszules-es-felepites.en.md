## What you will learn

- which questions to start planning a talk with,
- how to phrase a **key message**,
- the **introduction – body – conclusion** structure,
- how to use **storytelling**.

## Three questions before the slides

Don't start with PowerPoint! First answer these:

1. **Who is the audience?** What do they already know, what interests them, what are they worried about? (A faculty committee, an investor and a first-year student want to hear different things.)
2. **What is your goal?** To inform, persuade, ask for a decision, teach?
3. **What is the key message?** If the audience takes home a single sentence, what should it be?

> The key message test: can you say it in 30 seconds, in a lift? If not, you don't yet know what your talk is about.

## The three-part structure

| Part             | Share   | Content                                             |
| ---------------- | ------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **introduction** | ~10–15% | grab attention, topic and goal, what they will hear |
| **body**         | ~70–80% | 3–5 main ideas in a logical order, with examples    |
| **conclusion**   | ~10%    | summary, repeat the key message, call to action     |

The old orator's advice still works: _"Tell them what you're going to tell them; tell them; tell them what you told them."_

### Logical order in the body

- **chronological** (the story of the project),
- **problem – solution – result**,
- **general to specific**,
- **by importance**.

## A strong opening

Avoid: "Hi everyone, well, so I'm going to talk about our project now…"

Instead:

- a surprising **fact**: "40% of users give up at registration."
- a **question** to the audience: "Who forgot a password in the past week?"
- a short **story** or **problem**.

## A strong closing

Don't let the "Thank you for your attention, questions?" slide be the final thought. Close with the key message and a concrete **call to action** ("Try the beta, here's the QR code.").

## Storytelling

People remember stories, not bullet lists. A simple structure:

```text
situation → "A small webshop exported orders by hand every night."
conflict  → "Once a bug made 300 orders disappear."
solution  → "We wrote an automatic, verified synchronisation."
result    → "Since then 0 lost orders, 5 hours saved per week."
```

This is the **STAR** structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result), which also works well in job interviews.

## Summary

- First the audience, the goal and the key message, only then the slides.
- Structure: introduction – body (3–5 main ideas) – conclusion.
- Open strongly (fact, question, story), close with the key message and a call to action.
- A story (situation, conflict, solution, result) is more memorable than a list.
