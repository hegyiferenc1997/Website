## Note-taking

### The Cornell method

You divide the page into three parts:

```text
┌──────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Keywords,    │ Notes during the lecture            │
│ questions    │ (in your own words, briefly)        │
│ (afterwards) │                                     │
│              │                                     │
├──────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Summary in 2–3 sentences (afterwards)              │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

After the lecture you write **questions** in the left column – later you can quiz yourself with them (active recall!) while covering the right side.

### Mind maps

Related concepts branch out from a central concept. Good for an overview and for understanding the structure of a topic.

### By hand or on a computer?

Typing is faster, but we tend to transcribe everything word for word. What matters isn't the tool but that you **process** it: in your own words, briefly, marking connections.

## Sleep, exercise, breaks

- **Sleep**: during sleep the brain **organises and consolidates** what you learned during the day. An all-nighter before an exam impairs recall and thinking.
- **Exercise**: regular physical activity improves attention, mood and memory.
- **Breaks**: attention doesn't last for hours; short breaks (see Pomodoro, topic 14) keep you fresh. Don't scroll your phone during the break – stand up, walk, drink water instead.

## How to learn programming

Programming is a **skill**, not just knowledge – you learn it like an instrument: by **practising**.

- **Write code every day**, even if only for 20 minutes.
- **Type in** examples, don't paste them – and modify them: "what happens if…?"
- **Read error messages**: an error isn't a failure, it's information.
- **Small projects**: one idea of your own teaches more than ten videos ("tutorial hell": you watch many videos but can't start anything on your own).
- **Read other people's code** (open source, code review).
- If you're stuck: explain it (rubber duck), break it down, search for the exact error message, and only then ask – describe in your question what you've tried.

## Study groups

- **Explain** the material to each other – teaching is one of the best ways to learn.
- **Quiz** each other.
- Give the meeting a **goal and a time limit**, otherwise it turns into chatting.
- For assignments, follow the **rules**: discussing together is different from copying.

## Artificial intelligence as a study partner

AI tools can help learning a lot – or harm it a lot, depending on how you use them.

| Helps learning                                                          | Harms learning                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| having a concept explained in several ways                              | having the whole task solved, without thinking |
| generating practice questions, being quizzed                            | copying the answer without understanding       |
| having your own solution reviewed, looking for errors                   | believing everything without checking          |
| asking for a hint, not a solution ("give me a direction, not the code") | using it where the rules forbid it             |

> If the AI solves the practice problem for you, the practice – and with it the learning – doesn't happen. Use it like a patient tutor who asks and explains, not like an answer key.

## Summary

- Cornell notes: notes, questions added afterwards, summary – use the questions to quiz yourself.
- Without sleep, exercise and breaks learning is less effective.
- You learn programming by practising: daily coding, your own projects, reading error messages.
- In a study group, explain and quiz each other; AI should be a study partner, not an answer key.
