## What you will learn

- what **culture** means and why it resembles an **iceberg**,
- **Hofstede's** six cultural dimensions,
- how to use them **carefully**, without stereotypes.

## What is culture?

**Culture** is the set of values, norms, beliefs and behaviour patterns a group has learned together. It isn't only national: there is **organisational** culture (a startup vs. a bank), **professional** culture (engineers vs. marketers) and **generational** culture too.

### The iceberg model

```text
        ~~~~~~~ visible ~~~~~~~
          language, food, clothing,
          holidays, greetings
   ──────────────── waterline ────────────────
          communication style, view of time,
          attitude to authority and work,
          values, beliefs, basic assumptions
        ~~~~~~~ hidden ~~~~~~~
```

Most misunderstandings come from the part **below** the water: what the other person takes as "natural".

## Hofstede's six dimensions

Dutch researcher Geert Hofstede described differences between countries with dimensions, based on a survey of IBM employees:

| Dimension                                           | What it measures                                                               | Effect at work                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **power distance**                                  | how far unequal power distribution is accepted                                 | high: the boss decides, contradicting is improper; low: first names, flat hierarchy |
| **individualism – collectivism**                    | whether the individual or the group comes first                                | individualist: individual achievement; collectivist: team loyalty, harmony          |
| **masculinity – femininity** (competition vs. care) | whether success and competition or quality of life and cooperation matter more | competitive: performance-oriented; caring: consensus, balance                       |
| **uncertainty avoidance**                           | how much uncertainty and ambiguity bother people                               | high: detailed rules, specifications; low: flexibility, experimentation             |
| **long- or short-term orientation**                 | investing in the future or tradition, quick results                            | long-term: perseverance, saving; short-term: quick results                          |
| **indulgence – restraint**                          | how freely desires and fun can be enjoyed                                      | indulgent: optimism, leisure; restrained: stricter norms                            |

> Example: a developer from a low power-distance country (e.g. Denmark) openly argues with the manager in a meeting. A colleague from a high power-distance culture (e.g. Malaysia) may see this as disrespectful – and might not speak up even when they see a flaw in the plan.

## Careful with the dimensions

- The dimensions describe **national averages**, not individuals. Within one country the spread can be larger than between two countries.
- A **stereotype** is applying the average to every individual ("Germans are all…"). It leads to bad decisions.
- Use them as a **hypothesis**: if a reaction seems strange, consider a cultural cause – and **ask**.

## What can you do in an international team?

- Discuss the **way of working** explicitly (e.g. how we give feedback, what a deadline means).
- Offer **several channels** for contributing (also in writing), so quieter people speak up too.
- Avoid **slang, irony and local references**.
- Be curious, not judgemental.

## Summary

- Culture is jointly learned values and norms; most of it is hidden (iceberg).
- Hofstede: power distance, individualism, competition–care, uncertainty avoidance, time orientation, indulgence.
- The dimensions are averages, not individual traits – avoid stereotypes.
