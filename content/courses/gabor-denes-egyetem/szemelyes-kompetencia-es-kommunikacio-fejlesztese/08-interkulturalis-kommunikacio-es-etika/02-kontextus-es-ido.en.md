## What you will learn

- **high**- and **low-context** communication,
- the difference between **direct** and **indirect** feedback,
- **monochronic** and **polychronic** views of time,
- how to **adapt** in an international team.

## High and low context

According to anthropologist Edward T. Hall, cultures also differ in how much meaning lies in the **spoken words** and how much in the **situation** (relationship, tone, who says it, when).

| Low context                        | High context                                      |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| meaning is in the words            | meaning is also in the situation and relationship |
| direct, unambiguous                | indirect, nuanced, "between the lines"            |
| written contracts, detailed specs  | trust, personal relationships, verbal agreements  |
| e.g. Germany, the Netherlands, USA | e.g. Japan, China, Arab countries                 |

> In a high-context culture "we still need to consider this" may be a polite **no**. A low-context colleague takes it literally and follows up a week later.

## Direct and indirect feedback

According to Erin Meyer (_The Culture Map_), the directness of feedback doesn't always match context: for example the Dutch are generally direct with negative feedback, while in many Asian cultures voicing negative criticism publicly means **losing face**.

Tips:

- Give negative feedback **one-on-one**.
- If you come from a direct culture, **soften** it ("I think this part could be strengthened…").
- If you come from an indirect culture and write to direct colleagues, state the point **clearly**.
- If you're unsure what a reply meant, **ask back** concretely: "So the Friday deadline holds, or should we change it?"

## Views of time

| Monochronic                                 | Polychronic                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| one thing at a time, on schedule            | several things in parallel, flexibly                         |
| punctuality is a sign of respect            | relationships matter more than the schedule                  |
| the deadline is sacred                      | the deadline is a guideline                                  |
| e.g. Switzerland, Germany, Nordic countries | e.g. Latin American, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern countries |

Neither is "better" – but in a joint project you must **agree** on what a deadline and a meeting start time mean for you.

## Time zones and language

- Schedule meetings at a time that is working hours for **everyone** – and **rotate** who has to get up early.
- Write dates **unambiguously**: 2026-11-12 (ISO 8601) or "12 Nov 2026", because 11/12/2026 is 12 November in the USA but 11 December in Europe.
- Give the **time zone** with times (e.g. 15:00 CET).
- If English is the shared language, speak **more slowly** with simpler sentences; non-native speakers understand each other better than they understand native speakers.

## Summary

- Low context: meaning is in the words; high context: also in the situation.
- The directness of negative feedback varies by culture – one-on-one, adapted.
- Monochronic: one thing, punctuality; polychronic: parallelism, flexibility.
- In international teams: unambiguous dates and time zones, fair meeting times, simple English.
